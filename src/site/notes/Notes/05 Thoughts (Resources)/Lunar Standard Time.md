# 🌙 Lunar Standard Time — LST

## 1. Definition

**Lunar Standard Time (LST)** is a proposed global civil and rotational time standard for the Moon.

LST uses the **SI second** as its fundamental physical unit, but defines larger civil units specifically for the lunar environment.

The complete system is:

- 1 SI second = 1 second
- 1 Lunar Minute = 1,261 s
- 1 Lunar Hour = 52 Lunar Minutes = 65,572 s
- 1 Lunar Day = 36 Lunar Hours = 2,360,592 s
- 36 Lunar Hours/day
- 52 Lunar Minutes/hour
- 1,261 SI seconds/Lunar Minute
- 1 Lunar Hour = 10° longitude
- 0° = Lunar Standard Meridian
- 180° = Lunar Date Line
- 36 time zones, each 10°
- 1 Lunar Year = 360 Lunar Days
- 12 months/year
- 30 Lunar Days/month
- No leap years
- LST = global rotational standard
- Rule 1 = every 100 Lunar Days, apply −49 SI seconds

---

# 2. Fundamental SI Unit

LST does not redefine the SI second.

$$
\boxed{1\ \mathrm{SI\ second}=1\ \mathrm{second}}
$$

The SI second remains the fundamental physical unit from which the entire LST system is constructed.

This means that LST remains compatible with:

- atomic clocks,
- spacecraft clocks,
- computers,
- telecommunications,
- scientific instruments,
- navigation systems,
- terrestrial UTC-based systems.

LST therefore changes the **civil subdivisions of time**, not the physical definition of the second.

---

# 3. Lunar Minute

The first LST civil unit above the SI second is the **Lunar Minute**.

$$
\boxed{1\ \mathrm{Lunar\ Minute}=1,261\ \mathrm{s}}
$$

Therefore:

$$
1\ \mathrm{Lunar\ Minute}=1,261\ \mathrm{SI\ seconds}
$$

For comparison:

$$
1,261\ \mathrm{s}
=
21\ \mathrm{min}\ 1\ \mathrm{s}
$$

A Lunar Minute is therefore much longer than an Earth minute.

The important point is that the Lunar Minute is a defined civil unit and is not intended to equal 60 SI seconds.

---

# 4. Lunar Hour

One Lunar Hour consists of exactly 52 Lunar Minutes.

$$
\boxed{1\ \mathrm{Lunar\ Hour}=52\ \mathrm{Lunar\ Minutes}}
$$

Since:

$$
1\ \mathrm{Lunar\ Minute}=1,261\ \mathrm{s}
$$

we obtain:

$$
52\times1,261
=
65,572
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{1\ \mathrm{Lunar\ Hour}=65,572\ \mathrm{s}}
$$

In terrestrial SI-based time:

$$
65,572\ \mathrm{s}
=
18\ \mathrm{h}\ 12\ \mathrm{min}\ 52\ \mathrm{s}
$$

Thus a Lunar Hour is deliberately much longer than an Earth hour.

---

# 5. Lunar Day

One Lunar Day contains exactly 36 Lunar Hours.

$$
\boxed{1\ \mathrm{Lunar\ Day}=36\ \mathrm{Lunar\ Hours}}
$$

Since:

$$
1\ \mathrm{Lunar\ Hour}=65,572\ \mathrm{s}
$$

we obtain:

$$
36\times65,572
=
2,360,592
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{1\ \mathrm{Lunar\ Day}=2,360,592\ \mathrm{s}}
$$

The complete hierarchy is:

$$
1\ \mathrm{Lunar\ Day}
=
36\ \mathrm{Lunar\ Hours}
$$

$$
1\ \mathrm{Lunar\ Hour}
=
52\ \mathrm{Lunar\ Minutes}
$$

$$
1\ \mathrm{Lunar\ Minute}
=
1,261\ \mathrm{SI\ seconds}
$$

Therefore:

$$
1\ \mathrm{Lunar\ Day}
=
36\times52
=
1,872\ \mathrm{Lunar\ Minutes}
$$

and:

$$
1,872\times1,261
=
2,360,592\ \mathrm{s}
$$

---

# 6. The 36-Hour Lunar Day

LST defines:

$$
\boxed{36\ \mathrm{Lunar\ Hours/day}}
$$

The choice of 36 hours is directly connected to lunar longitude.

The Moon has a full 360° longitude system.

Dividing the circumference into 36 equal temporal divisions gives:

$$
\frac{360^\circ}{36}
=
10^\circ
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{1\ \mathrm{Lunar\ Hour}=10^\circ}
$$

This is one of the central principles of LST.

The clock is therefore simultaneously a temporal and geographical system.

# 8. Lunar Standard Meridian

The global reference longitude is:

$$
\boxed{0^\circ}
$$

This is called the:

**Lunar Standard Meridian (LSM)**

The LSM is the geographical origin of the LST time-zone system.

The global LST clock is defined at this reference meridian.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{0^\circ=LST+0}
$$

Every other lunar local time is derived from this reference.

---

# 9. Lunar Date Line

The opposite longitude is:

$$
\boxed{180^\circ}
$$

This is the:

**Lunar Date Line (LDL)**

It is the principal civil boundary at which the Lunar Date changes.

The relationship is:

$$
0^\circ
\longrightarrow
180^\circ
\longrightarrow
360^\circ
$$

with:

$$
360^\circ\equiv0^\circ
$$

The Lunar Date Line therefore divides the lunar globe into two calendar sides.

---

# 10. LST Time Zones

LST contains:

$$
\boxed{36\ \mathrm{nominal\ time\ zones}}
$$

Each nominal zone covers:

$$
\boxed{10^\circ}
$$

Therefore:

$$
36\times10^\circ=360^\circ
$$

Each time zone corresponds to one Lunar Hour.

Thus:

$$
\boxed{1\ \mathrm{time\ zone}=1\ \mathrm{Lunar\ Hour}}
$$

The complete global system therefore contains:

$$
\boxed{36\ \mathrm{hours}=36\ \mathrm{zones}=360^\circ}
$$

---

# 11. Global Rotational Standard

LST is defined as a:

$$
\boxed{\text{global rotational standard}}
$$

This means that LST is not merely a local time used by one lunar settlement.

It is intended to be the common reference timescale for the entire Moon.

The architecture is:

```text
                    LST
                     │
          Lunar Standard Meridian
                     │
        ┌────────────┼────────────┐
        │            │            │
     LST−x        LST+0        LST+x
        │            │            │
   Local Time    Reference    Local Time
```

Every lunar settlement can maintain a local civil time while remaining synchronized with the same global LST reference.

# 12. Lunar Calendar

The LST calendar contains:

$$
\boxed{360\ \mathrm{Lunar\ Days/year}}
$$

The year contains:

$$
\boxed{12\ \mathrm{months}}
$$

Every month contains:

$$
\boxed{30\ \mathrm{Lunar\ Days}}
$$

Therefore:

$$
12\times30
=
360
$$

The calendar is intentionally regular.

There are:

- no 31-day months,
- no 28-day months,
- no 29-day months,
- no leap months,
- no leap years.

---

# 13. No Leap Years

LST uses:

$$
\boxed{\text{No leap years}}
$$

Every year is exactly:

$$
360\ \mathrm{Lunar\ Days}
$$

Therefore the civil calendar does not contain a special additional day.

The year structure is permanently:

$$
30+30+30+30+30+30+30+30+30+30+30+30
$$

or:

$$
\boxed{12\times30=360}
$$

This makes calendar arithmetic highly predictable.

# 16. LST Epoch

The LST system begins at the following historical epoch:

$$
\boxed{\text{21 July 1969, 02:56:15 UTC}}
$$

This is the exact epoch specified for the system.

In ISO 8601 form:

$$
\boxed{\texttt{1969-07-21T02:56:15Z}}
$$

This instant is:

**LST Epoch 0**

or:

**LST T0**

The epoch is tied to the Apollo 11 lunar-landing era and provides a fixed historical origin for the LST timescale.

---

# 17. Epoch Definition

The formal definition is:

```text
LST Epoch (T₀)
= 1969-07-21T02:56:15Z
```

At the epoch:

$$
\boxed{T_{\mathrm{LST}}=0}
$$

The epoch therefore establishes the zero point from which all subsequent LST elapsed time can be calculated.

A timestamp after the epoch is determined by calculating its elapsed SI time from:

$$
\boxed{\texttt{1969-07-21T02:56:15Z}}
$$

and then converting that elapsed time into LST Lunar Days, Lunar Hours, Lunar Minutes, and SI seconds.

---

# 19. Epoch Conversion

Let:

$$
T_{\mathrm{UTC}}
$$

be an instant expressed as an SI-based UTC timestamp.

Let:

$$
T_0
=
\texttt{1969-07-21T02:56:15Z}
$$

Then the elapsed physical time from the epoch is:

$$
\Delta T
=
T_{\mathrm{UTC}}-T_0
$$

The nominal number of Lunar Days elapsed is:

$$
D
=
\left\lfloor
\frac{\Delta T}{2,360,592}
\right\rfloor
$$

The remaining time is:

$$
R
=
\Delta T
-
D(2,360,592)
$$

Then:

$$
H
=
\left\lfloor
\frac{R}{65,572}
\right\rfloor
$$

The remaining time after the Lunar Hour is:

$$
R_H
=
R-H(65,572)
$$

Then:

$$
M
=
\left\lfloor
\frac{R_H}{1,261}
\right\rfloor
$$

and the remaining quantity is expressed directly in SI seconds.

# 20. Epoch Correction Rule

The epoch establishes the starting point.

The periodic astronomical correction does not change the epoch.

Instead, the correction modifies the relationship between the ideal civil clock and the physical rotational reference after each 100-Lunar-Day interval.

The basic sequence is:

```text
LST Epoch
     │
     ▼
100 Lunar Days
     │
     ▼
−49 SI seconds
     │
     ▼
200 Lunar Days
     │
     ▼
−49 SI seconds
     │
     ▼
300 Lunar Days
     │
     ▼
−49 SI seconds
     │
     ▼
...
```

The correction schedule is continuous and does not reset at the beginning of a calendar year.

---

# 21. Rule 1 — Negative Rotational Correction

The first and currently defined correction rule is:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Every 100 Lunar Days, apply }-49\ \mathrm{SI\ seconds}
}
$$

This is a **negative rotational correction**.

The purpose is to compensate for the difference between the fixed civil Lunar Day and the adopted lunar rotational model.

The civil day is:

$$
2,360,592\ \mathrm{s}
$$

while the adopted rotational model is approximately:

$$
2,360,591.5104\ \mathrm{s}
$$

The difference is:

$$
2,360,592-2,360,591.5104
=
0.4896\ \mathrm{s}
$$

per Lunar Day.

---

# 22. Accumulated Drift

Over 100 Lunar Days:

$$
100\times0.4896
=
48.96\ \mathrm{s}
$$

Therefore the nominal civil clock accumulates approximately:

$$
\boxed{48.96\ \mathrm{s}}
$$

of rotational offset per 100 Lunar Days.

Rule 1 removes:

$$
49\ \mathrm{s}
$$

Therefore:

$$
48.96-49
=
-0.04\ \mathrm{s}
$$

The residual after each complete 100-day correction interval is therefore:

$$
\boxed{-0.04\ \mathrm{s}}
$$

under the adopted rotational model.

---

# 23. Correction Is Not a Leap Year

The −49-second correction is completely separate from the calendar.

It does not:

- add a Lunar Day,
- remove a Lunar Day,
- change a month,
- create a leap year,
- alter the 30-day month structure.

The calendar always remains:

$$
360\ \mathrm{Lunar\ Days/year}
$$

The correction operates on the continuous time scale.

---

# 24. Correction Schedule

The correction events occur after every 100 Lunar Days:

$$
100,\ 200,\ 300,\ 400,\ 500,\ 600,\ldots
$$

Because the calendar year contains 360 Lunar Days, the first three corrections of a calendar year occur at:

$$
100,\ 200,\ 300
$$

Lunar Days after the beginning of that year.

The fourth 100-day interval crosses the year boundary.

For example:

$$
300+60=360
$$

completes one Lunar Year.

The next correction occurs at:

$$
400-360=40
$$

days into the next Lunar Year.

Thus the astronomical correction cycle is continuous.

---

# 25. Long-Term Correction

After $N$ complete correction intervals:

$$
\Delta T_{\mathrm{correction}}
=
-49N\ \mathrm{s}
$$

The nominal accumulated rotational difference is:

$$
48.96N\ \mathrm{s}
$$

Therefore the residual is:

$$
48.96N-49N
=
-0.04N\ \mathrm{s}
$$

Thus Rule 1 is a very close approximation to the adopted rotational model, but the correction amount is deliberately specified as the integer value:

$$
\boxed{-49\ \mathrm{s}}
$$

rather than:

$$
-48.96\ \mathrm{s}
$$

Mathematically:

$$
36\times52=1,872
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{1\ \mathrm{Lunar\ Day}=1,872\ \mathrm{Lunar\ Minutes}}
$$

# 39. LST and UTC

UTC and LST serve different purposes.

### UTC

The primary terrestrial civil timescale.

### LST

The proposed lunar global rotational civil timescale.

The relationship begins from:

```text
LST Epoch = 1969-07-21T02:56:15Z
```

After that epoch, LST progresses according to its own defined civil units and its periodic rotational corrections.

Therefore LST is not simply a fixed UTC offset.

It is an independent timescale with its own:

- day length,
- hour length,
- minute length,
- calendar,
- longitude system,
- date line,
- correction rules.
