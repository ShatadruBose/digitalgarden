# Hexagons Are The Bestagons
## A Comprehensive Paper on the Geometry, Mathematics, Science, Engineering, Nature, Culture, and Philosophy of the Hexagon

---

## Abstract

The hexagon is one of the most remarkable shapes in geometry. Defined simply as a polygon with six sides and six angles, it appears deceptively ordinary. Yet the hexagon occupies an extraordinary position across mathematics, physics, chemistry, biology, engineering, architecture, computer science, art, design, and nature. Its geometry combines symmetry, efficiency, structural stability, tiling capability, and visual balance in a way that makes it unusually useful.

The phrase **“Hexagons Are The Bestagons”** is humorous, but it points toward a surprisingly serious mathematical observation: six-sided structures repeatedly emerge when systems attempt to optimize competing requirements such as area, perimeter, packing density, material usage, stability, and symmetry.

Regular hexagons can tile an infinite plane without gaps or overlaps. They possess sixfold rotational symmetry, six reflection axes, and a highly efficient relationship between boundary length and enclosed area. Three regular hexagons meet neatly around every vertex, creating a continuous honeycomb. This same geometry appears naturally in beehives, basalt formations, molecular structures, crystal lattices, graphene, snowflakes, architectural systems, games, maps, icons, and countless human-designed objects.

The hexagon is therefore more than a polygon. It is a recurring solution to geometric problems.

This paper explores why.

---

# 1. Introduction

Geometry contains many shapes.

There are triangles with three sides, quadrilaterals with four, pentagons with five, hexagons with six, heptagons with seven, octagons with eight, and polygons with arbitrarily many sides.

Every polygon has its own mathematical properties.

Yet some shapes have become particularly important because they interact exceptionally well with the physical and mathematical world.

The hexagon is one of them.

A hexagon is any polygon with six sides. The sides may have different lengths, the angles may differ, and the shape may be convex or concave. Among all hexagons, the **regular hexagon** is particularly important.

A regular hexagon has:

- six equal sides;
- six equal interior angles;
- six equal exterior angles;
- sixfold rotational symmetry;
- six reflection axes;
- a simple relationship to the equilateral triangle;
- the ability to tile a plane perfectly;
- excellent packing characteristics;
- and a strong structural geometry.

Its interior angle is:

\[
\frac{(6-2)180^\circ}{6}=120^\circ.
\]

Thus every interior angle of a regular hexagon is:

\[
120^\circ.
\]

Its exterior angle is:

\[
180^\circ-120^\circ=60^\circ.
\]

That \(60^\circ\) angle is significant because it is also the fundamental angle associated with equilateral triangles and sixfold rotational symmetry.

This gives the regular hexagon a deep connection with triangular geometry.

A regular hexagon can be divided into six equilateral triangles by drawing segments from its center to each vertex.

Therefore:

> **The regular hexagon is essentially six equilateral triangles organized around a single central point.**

This simple fact explains much of its mathematical behavior.

---

# 2. What Is a Hexagon?

The word *hexagon* comes from Greek roots:

- **hex** — six;
- **gon** — angle or corner.

Thus, a hexagon literally means a six-angled polygon.

A polygon is a closed two-dimensional figure consisting of straight line segments.

A hexagon therefore consists of six connected line segments.

Let the vertices be:

\[
A,B,C,D,E,F.
\]

The sides are:

\[
AB,\ BC,\ CD,\ DE,\ EF,\ FA.
\]

The polygon closes after six sides.

---

# 3. Types of Hexagons

Not every hexagon looks like a honeycomb cell.

There are many possible hexagons.

## 3.1 Convex Hexagon

A convex hexagon has no indentation.

Every diagonal lies entirely inside the polygon.

Its interior angles are all less than \(180^\circ\).

---

## 3.2 Concave Hexagon

A concave hexagon has at least one interior angle greater than \(180^\circ\).

It has an indentation.

Concave hexagons can be extremely irregular, but they remain six-sided polygons.

---

## 3.3 Regular Hexagon

The regular hexagon is the most famous member of the family.

All six sides are equal:

\[
a=a=a=a=a=a.
\]

All six angles are equal:

\[
120^\circ.
\]

It is simultaneously:

- equilateral;
- equiangular;
- cyclic;
- highly symmetrical;
- and capable of regular tessellation.

---

## 3.4 Irregular Hexagon

An irregular hexagon does not have all sides and angles equal.

Examples can be found throughout nature and engineering.

A honeycomb cell may look approximately regular, but real biological structures contain imperfections.

# 4. The Geometry of the Regular Hexagon

The regular hexagon has a remarkably elegant geometry.

Suppose its side length is \(a\).

Its perimeter is:

\[
P=6a.
\]

Its area is:

\[
A=\frac{3\sqrt3}{2}a^2.
\]

This formula comes directly from its decomposition into six equilateral triangles.

The area of one equilateral triangle with side \(a\) is:

\[
A_t=\frac{\sqrt3}{4}a^2.
\]

There are six such triangles:

\[
A=6\left(\frac{\sqrt3}{4}a^2\right).
\]

Therefore:

\[
A=\frac{6\sqrt3}{4}a^2
\]

and hence:

\[
\boxed{A=\frac{3\sqrt3}{2}a^2}.
\]

This is one of the simplest and most beautiful area formulas in polygon geometry.

---

# 5. Hexagons and the Number Six

The number six is unusually compatible with geometry.

Six can be divided by:

\[
1,\ 2,\ 3,\ 6.
\]

More importantly, \(360^\circ\) divides perfectly into six equal rotations:

\[
\frac{360^\circ}{6}=60^\circ.
\]

That produces the six directions of a regular hexagonal lattice.

Starting from one vertex direction and rotating by \(60^\circ\) repeatedly gives:

\[
0^\circ,
60^\circ,
120^\circ,
180^\circ,
240^\circ,
300^\circ.
\]

After six rotations, we return to:

\[
360^\circ.
\]

This is why hexagonal systems naturally produce six-directional geometry.

---

# 6. The Sixfold Symmetry of the Hexagon

A regular hexagon has **dihedral symmetry \(D_6\)**.

It has:

- 6 rotational symmetries;
- 6 reflection symmetries.

Thus it has 12 symmetry operations in total.

The rotational symmetries are:

\[
0^\circ,\ 60^\circ,\ 120^\circ,\ 180^\circ,\ 240^\circ,\ 300^\circ.
\]

The existence of sixfold symmetry makes hexagons visually stable and mathematically rich.

Rotating a regular hexagon by \(60^\circ\) produces exactly the same shape.

That is not true for most polygons.

A square requires \(90^\circ\).

An equilateral triangle requires \(120^\circ\).

A pentagon requires \(72^\circ\).

A regular hexagon requires only:

\[
60^\circ.
\]

---

# 7. Hexagons and Triangles

One of the deepest reasons the hexagon is mathematically convenient is its relationship with the equilateral triangle.

Draw lines from the center of a regular hexagon to all six vertices.

The hexagon becomes six equilateral triangles.

Therefore:

\[
\text{Hexagon}=6\times\text{Equilateral Triangle}.
\]

This means that hexagonal geometry can frequently be analyzed using triangular geometry.

The center-to-vertex distance equals the side length:

\[
R=a.
\]

The apothem is:

\[
r=\frac{\sqrt3}{2}a.
\]

The relationship is:

\[
r=\frac{\sqrt3}{2}R.
\]

This is another elegant consequence of the \(60^\circ\) geometry.

# 8. Why Hexagons Tile the Plane

One of the greatest powers of the regular hexagon is its ability to tessellate.

A tessellation is a repeated pattern that covers a surface without:

- gaps;
- overlaps.

Three regular hexagons meet at every vertex.

Each contributes:

\[
120^\circ.
\]

Therefore:

\[
120^\circ+120^\circ+120^\circ=360^\circ.
\]

The entire plane can therefore be covered.

This produces the familiar honeycomb pattern.

Among the regular polygons, only three can independently tile the Euclidean plane:

1. equilateral triangles;
2. squares;
3. regular hexagons.

Their interior angles are:

\[
60^\circ,\ 90^\circ,\ 120^\circ.
\]

The relevant vertex combinations are:

### Triangles

\[
6(60^\circ)=360^\circ
\]

### Squares

\[
4(90^\circ)=360^\circ
\]

### Hexagons

\[
3(120^\circ)=360^\circ
\]

The hexagon therefore sits at one of the three fundamental regular tessellating geometries.

# 9. The Honeycomb

The honeycomb is perhaps the most famous natural application of hexagonal geometry.

Honeybees construct cells in which they:

- store honey;
- store pollen;
- raise larvae.

The cells are approximately hexagonal.

A honeycomb consists of enormous numbers of adjacent cells arranged in a repeating pattern.

This arrangement provides several advantages.

It:

- fills space efficiently;
- minimizes wasted gaps;
- creates strong walls;
- uses relatively little wax;
- produces a compact storage system.

The honeycomb has therefore become a symbol of mathematical efficiency.

# 10. Why Bees Use Hexagons

The exact evolutionary development of honeycomb geometry is biologically complex.

However, from a geometric perspective, the hexagon has an obvious advantage.

Suppose identical cells must fill a flat region.

Circles have excellent area-to-perimeter efficiency individually, but circles cannot tile the plane without gaps.

Squares tile the plane, but their geometry has more exposed boundary per enclosed area than a hexagonal arrangement.

Regular hexagons combine:

1. efficient enclosure;
2. perfect tessellation;
3. structural regularity.

This makes them extremely attractive for a biological construction system.

---

# 11. The Honeycomb Conjecture

The mathematical significance of honeycomb geometry became even more profound through the **Honeycomb Conjecture**.

The central problem asks:

> How can a plane be divided into regions of equal area while minimizing the total perimeter?

The answer is the regular hexagonal honeycomb.

The conjecture was proposed by L. Fejes Tóth in the twentieth century and was finally proved by Thomas C. Hales in 1999.

The result establishes mathematically what the honeycomb suggests visually:

> **Among partitions of the plane into equal-area regions, the regular hexagonal honeycomb minimizes total perimeter.**

This is a spectacular example of a natural pattern corresponding to an optimization principle.

# 12. Hexagons as an Optimization Solution

The importance of the hexagon is not simply that it looks nice.

It frequently appears because systems are optimizing something.

Consider a problem with three objectives:

\[
\text{maximize area}
\]

while simultaneously:

\[
\text{minimize boundary}
\]

and:

\[
\text{avoid empty space}.
\]

A circle is excellent for the first two conditions but cannot tile a plane.

A square tiles perfectly but is not as perimeter-efficient.

The regular hexagon provides a powerful compromise.

This is one reason hexagonal structures repeatedly appear in engineering and nature.

# 13. Hexagonal Packing

Packing problems ask how objects can be arranged efficiently.

Hexagonal arrangements often achieve extremely high packing densities.

Consider circles arranged on a flat surface.

The densest arrangement is the triangular lattice, which can also be viewed as a hexagonal arrangement around each circle.

Each circle has six nearest neighbors.

The resulting pattern contains repeated sixfold local structure.

This appears in:

- molecular systems;
- granular materials;
- colloids;
- particle simulations;
- crystal structures;
- computational geometry.

# 14. Hexagonal Close Packing

In three dimensions, hexagonal geometry becomes even more important.

**Hexagonal close packing (HCP)** is one of the fundamental arrangements of equal spheres.

Its layers follow an:

\[
ABABAB\ldots
\]

pattern.

Each layer has a hexagonal arrangement.

The coordination number is:

\[
12.
\]

That means each sphere can have twelve nearest neighbors in the ideal close-packed structure.

The packing fraction is:

\[
\eta=\frac{\pi}{3\sqrt2}.
\]

Numerically:

\[
\eta\approx0.74048.
\]

Thus approximately \(74.05\%\) of the volume is occupied by spheres.

The remaining space consists of voids between them.

# 16. Graphene

One of the most famous modern hexagonal structures is graphene.

Graphene is a single layer of carbon atoms arranged in a two-dimensional honeycomb lattice.

Its structure consists of carbon atoms connected in a repeating hexagonal network.

Graphene is famous for its:

- exceptional electrical conductivity;
- high mechanical strength;
- low mass;
- thermal conductivity;
- unusual electronic properties.

The hexagonal arrangement is fundamental to its physics.

The carbon atoms form a network that can be represented using a two-dimensional honeycomb lattice.

# 17. Graphene and the Honeycomb Lattice

The graphene lattice is often represented as hexagonal, although the mathematical distinction between the honeycomb graph and the underlying triangular Bravais lattice is important.

The atoms occupy two interpenetrating sublattices commonly called:

\[
A
\]

and:

\[
B.
\]

Each carbon atom has three nearest neighbors.

This creates a three-connected network of hexagonal faces.

The geometry strongly influences graphene's electronic band structure.

In particular, graphene's famous Dirac points arise from the symmetry and connectivity of its lattice.

Thus a simple six-sided polygon becomes connected to quantum mechanics and condensed-matter physics.

# 18. Carbon and Hexagonal Chemistry

Carbon chemistry contains numerous hexagonal motifs.

Benzene is the classic example.

Its six carbon atoms form a six-membered ring.

The simplified structural representation is a regular hexagon with alternating bonds, although the actual electronic structure involves delocalized \(\pi\) electrons rather than six independently localized double bonds.

Benzene has molecular formula:

\[
C_6H_6.
\]

The six carbon atoms form a planar ring.

This structure produces extraordinary stability associated with aromaticity.

Thus:

\[
6\text{-membered ring}
\rightarrow
\text{symmetry}
\rightarrow
\text{delocalization}
\rightarrow
\text{aromatic stability}.
\]

# 20. Cyclohexane

\[

> A hexagon may be mathematically flat while the physical system represented by it is three-dimensional.

# 22. Compound Eyes

# 23. Basalt Columns

Nature creates another spectacular hexagonal structure in volcanic rocks.

When certain lava flows cool and contract, fractures can propagate through the material.

Under suitable conditions, the cracks form polygonal columnar patterns.

Many of the resulting columns are approximately hexagonal.

One of the world's most famous examples is the Giant's Causeway in Northern Ireland.

Its columns are predominantly polygonal, with many exhibiting six sides.

This is not because nature possesses a hidden preference for drawing perfect hexagons.

Instead, the geometry emerges from physical fracture and contraction processes.

# 24. Why Cooling Can Produce Hexagons

Imagine a large volume of cooling material.

As it cools, it contracts.

If contraction creates a network of fractures, the cracks tend to distribute stresses.

A perfectly uniform system might produce idealized geometries, but real materials produce irregular polygons.

Hexagons often appear because they are an efficient local way to divide a plane into regions.

This is related to the same geometric principles behind cellular patterns.

# 25. Saturn's Hexagon

Perhaps the most astonishing hexagon in the Solar System is found on Saturn.

A persistent six-sided atmospheric pattern exists around Saturn's north polar region.

It is known simply as the:

**Saturn hexagon.**

Unlike a solid geometric object, it is an atmospheric wave pattern.

It extends around the planet's north pole.

Its persistence is one of the most fascinating examples of large-scale planetary fluid dynamics.

---

# 26. A Hexagon Larger Than Earth

The scale of Saturn's polar hexagon is extraordinary.

# 30. Hexagonal Grids in Mathematics

\[

In a square grid, diagonal movement has distance:

Hexagonal grids can reduce certain forms of grid anisotropy.

\frac{

# 39. Why Nuts Are Hexagonal

A hexagonal nut provides six flat faces.

A wrench can grip any of these faces.

After turning the nut through a relatively small angle, another face becomes accessible.

For a six-sided nut, the wrench can be repositioned every:

\[
60^\circ.
\]

This is convenient in confined spaces.

A square nut would require:

\[
90^\circ
\]

between equivalent orientations.

A hexagonal nut gives more frequent opportunities to reposition the wrench.

Thus:

\[
6\text{ sides}
\rightarrow
60^\circ\text{ increments}
\rightarrow
excellent wrench accessibility.
\]

# 46. The Isoperimetric Principle

The classical isoperimetric problem asks:

> Among all planar shapes with a fixed perimeter, which encloses the greatest area?

The answer is:

\[
\text{circle}.
\]

Therefore, it would be incorrect to claim that a hexagon is universally optimal.

The circle wins the pure area-perimeter problem.

But the circle cannot tile the plane without gaps.

That changes the problem.

Once we require:

\[
\text{repeated cells}
+
\text{no gaps}
+
\text{efficient boundaries},
\]

the hexagon becomes extraordinarily important.

This distinction is crucial.

---

# 47. The Hexagon Is Not Always the Best Shape

“Hexagons Are The Bestagons” is a fun slogan, not a universal theorem.

Different problems have different optimal solutions.

For example:

- circles are optimal for the planar isoperimetric problem;
- triangles are extremely efficient for triangulation;
- squares are excellent for Cartesian grids;
- rectangles are useful for construction;
- spheres minimize surface area for fixed volume;
- cylinders are useful for rotational storage;
- cones are useful for funnels.

The hexagon is powerful because it is **often exceptionally good**, not because it wins every possible mathematical competition.

# 48. Hexagons and Surface Area

A repeated hexagonal structure can provide an excellent surface-area-to-volume relationship.

For a prism with a regular hexagonal cross-section:

\[
A_{\text{base}}=\frac{3\sqrt3}{2}a^2.
\]

If the prism has height \(h\), then:

\[
V=\frac{3\sqrt3}{2}a^2h.
\]

Its lateral surface area is:

\[
L=6ah.
\]

Thus hexagonal prisms are useful geometric building blocks.

# 70. Hexagon Area Derivation

Let a regular hexagon have side length \(a\).

\[

\[

Simplifying:

\[

\frac{3\sqrt3}{2}a^2.

For:

\[

we obtain:

\[

\frac{3\sqrt3}{2}(4)
=
6\sqrt3.

Approximately:

\[
A\approx10.392.
