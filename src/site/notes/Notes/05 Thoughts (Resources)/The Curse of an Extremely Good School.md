# The Curse of an Extremely Good School
## Universal Education, the Disappearance of Cheap Labour, Automation, and the Transformation of the Economy

> **A thought experiment about what might happen if a school became so extraordinarily effective that almost every student became highly educated, highly skilled, highly productive, and capable of performing sophisticated intellectual work.**

---

# Abstract

Education is generally regarded as one of the most powerful mechanisms for improving individuals and societies. Better schools can produce better scientists, engineers, doctors, programmers, entrepreneurs, managers, researchers, artists, and skilled workers. A highly educated population tends to possess greater productivity, technological capability, and problem-solving capacity.

However, an interesting economic paradox appears when this idea is taken to its extreme.

Suppose a school becomes extraordinarily good—not merely good enough to improve examination results, but so effective that virtually every student develops advanced intellectual, technical, mathematical, scientific, linguistic, creative, and professional abilities. Suppose this effect spreads throughout an entire society.

At first glance, this seems unambiguously beneficial.

Yet such a transformation could create an unusual economic problem: **the supply of workers willing and available to perform low-paid, repetitive, undesirable, or low-skill work could decline dramatically.**

A society whose population becomes highly capable may experience increasing difficulty maintaining a large supply of cheap labour. Jobs such as cleaning, agricultural labour, basic manufacturing, food preparation, delivery, construction assistance, routine clerical work, and other low-wage occupations may become less attractive relative to skilled alternatives.

Employers would then have to compete for workers by increasing wages or improving working conditions. As labour becomes more expensive, businesses would face pressure to automate, redesign jobs, increase productivity, recruit workers from elsewhere, or pass higher costs to consumers.

This creates what can be called, for the purposes of this paper, the **"Curse of the Extremely Good School."**

The term is deliberately paradoxical. The "curse" is not that education is harmful. Rather, it describes a situation in which the success of an education system creates economic pressures that undermine an economic structure dependent upon abundant, inexpensive labour.

The phenomenon reveals a deeper principle:

> **An economy cannot indefinitely depend upon large quantities of cheap human labour while simultaneously making every member of its population highly skilled and economically productive.**

However, the paradox does not necessarily imply economic decline. In fact, the opposite may occur. Higher wages can stimulate automation, technological innovation, productivity growth, and the creation of entirely new industries. The economy may transition from a low-wage labour model to a high-productivity model.

Thus, the "curse" may ultimately be better understood as a **structural transition**.

---

# 1. Introduction

Education is usually discussed as an investment.

A student receives education.

The student develops knowledge and skills.

Those skills increase productivity.

Higher productivity allows the individual to earn more.

The economy becomes more productive.

Technological progress accelerates.

Living standards improve.

This is the conventional positive relationship:

\[
\text{Better Education}
\rightarrow
\text{Better Skills}
\rightarrow
\text{Higher Productivity}
\rightarrow
\text{Higher Income}
\]

But what happens if education becomes *extremely* effective?

Consider a hypothetical school.

It has exceptional teachers, extraordinary learning technology, excellent curriculum design, personalized education, powerful laboratories, abundant resources, excellent infrastructure, and an education system optimized for developing every student's potential.

Instead of producing a normal distribution of abilities, the school produces an unusually capable population.

Students graduate with:

- strong mathematical ability,
- scientific literacy,
- programming skills,
- communication skills,
- financial literacy,
- engineering knowledge,
- critical thinking,
- research ability,
- technological competence,
- entrepreneurship,
- creativity,
- foreign-language ability,
- professional discipline,
- and advanced problem-solving skills.

Now imagine that this is not one exceptional school.

Imagine that **every school in the country becomes like this.**

The result could be extraordinary economic development.

But something else happens.

The labour market changes.

If millions of people who previously would have entered low-skilled occupations instead become engineers, programmers, technicians, researchers, managers, entrepreneurs, healthcare professionals, and other highly skilled workers, the supply of people willing to perform low-paid work decreases.

The economy therefore encounters an unusual problem:

> **The better society becomes at educating people, the harder it may become to maintain an economy based on cheap human labour.**

This is the central paradox examined in this paper.

---

# 2. Defining the "Curse"

The phrase **"Curse of the Extremely Good School"** is not a standard economic law or established academic theory.

It is a conceptual name for a thought experiment.

The proposed mechanism is:

\[
\text{Exceptional Education}
\rightarrow
\text{Universal Skill Improvement}
\rightarrow
\text{Declining Supply of Low-Skill Labour}
\]

which can continue as:

\[
\rightarrow
\text{Rising Low-Skill Wages}
\rightarrow
\text{Higher Production Costs}
\rightarrow
\text{Automation Pressure}
\]

and potentially:

\[
\rightarrow
\text{Higher Productivity}
\rightarrow
\text{Economic Transformation}
\]

Therefore, the "curse" has two possible interpretations.

### Negative interpretation

The economy loses its supply of cheap labour.

### Positive interpretation

The economy is forced to become more productive.

This distinction is extremely important.

The disappearance of cheap labour is not necessarily equivalent to the disappearance of economic prosperity.

In fact:

\[
\boxed{\text{Expensive Labour} \neq \text{Weak Economy}}
\]

A country can have expensive workers and still have a highly productive economy.

---

# 3. The Basic Economic Model

Consider an economy with two broad categories of labour:

\[
L = L_S + L_U
\]

where:

- \(L_S\) = skilled labour
- \(L_U\) = lower-skilled or routine labour

Suppose the education system improves.

Some workers move from the second category toward the first:

\[
L_U \downarrow
\]

while:

\[
L_S \uparrow
\]

Initially, this looks beneficial.

However, if industries still require \(L_U\), a shortage develops.

For example, suppose an economy requires:

\[
10,000,000
\]

routine workers.

But after a massive educational transformation, only:

\[
7,000,000
\]

people are willing to perform such jobs at existing wages.

There is now a labour shortage:

\[
10,000,000 - 7,000,000
=
3,000,000
\]

workers.

Employers must respond.

They can:

1. increase wages,
2. improve working conditions,
3. automate,
4. redesign the job,
5. import labour,
6. reduce production,
7. raise prices,
8. increase productivity.

The market therefore begins adjusting.

---

# 4. Why Cheap Labour Exists

To understand the paradox, we must first understand what makes labour cheap.

A worker's wage is influenced by many factors, including:

- productivity,
- scarcity,
- bargaining power,
- skill requirements,
- working conditions,
- geographic location,
- legal institutions,
- labour demand,
- labour supply,
- alternative employment opportunities,
- technology,
- capital availability.

A simplified labour-market model can be expressed as:

\[
W = f(D_L,S_L)
\]

where:

- \(W\) = wage,
- \(D_L\) = demand for labour,
- \(S_L\) = supply of labour.

If many workers are available for a particular job, employers have less difficulty filling it.

If few workers are available, employers must compete more aggressively for workers.

Thus:

\[
S_L \downarrow
\Rightarrow
W \uparrow
\]

assuming demand remains reasonably strong.

This is the central economic mechanism behind the thought experiment.

---

# 5. The School Changes the Labour Supply Curve

Suppose a society has a large population of workers with limited formal education.

Many occupations may be available to them:

- agricultural labour,
- cleaning,
- warehouse work,
- factory assembly,
- construction assistance,
- basic transportation,
- domestic work,
- routine office work,
- retail,
- food preparation.

Now dramatically improve education.

Students who previously would have entered these occupations may instead qualify for:

- software engineering,
- medicine,
- engineering,
- scientific research,
- accounting,
- architecture,
- finance,
- management,
- cybersecurity,
- data science,
- design,
- entrepreneurship,
- skilled technical occupations.

The opportunity cost of taking a low-wage job increases.

If someone can earn substantially more as a technician than as a cleaner, they have an economic incentive to choose the technician position.

Therefore:

\[
\text{Alternative Opportunities} \uparrow
\Rightarrow
\text{Supply of Low-Wage Labour} \downarrow
\]

---

# 6. Opportunity Cost

Opportunity cost is fundamental to understanding the problem.

Imagine two workers.

## Worker A

Potential skilled job:

\[
₹60,000/\text{month}
\]

Potential low-skilled job:

\[
₹12,000/\text{month}
\]

Worker A is unlikely to voluntarily choose the ₹12,000 job unless there are other benefits.

Now suppose education becomes dramatically better.

Worker A acquires advanced technical skills.

The skilled opportunity becomes:

\[
₹100,000/\text{month}
\]

The low-skilled opportunity remains:

\[
₹12,000/\text{month}
\]

The opportunity cost of performing the low-skilled job has increased enormously.

The worker is effectively giving up:

\[
₹88,000
\]

per month relative to the alternative.

This makes the low-skilled occupation increasingly unattractive.

---

# 7. The "Everyone Becomes Skilled" Scenario

Now take the thought experiment to an extreme.

Imagine:

> **Every student leaves school highly capable.**

There are almost no academically weak students.

Almost everyone can:

- read complex material,
- perform advanced mathematics,
- use computers,
- write software,
- analyze information,
- learn new technologies,
- communicate effectively,
- operate sophisticated machinery,
- solve complex problems.

The labour market suddenly contains a massive amount of human capital.

This creates an unusual situation.

The supply of skilled labour is extremely high.

But the supply of workers willing to accept low-skilled jobs is comparatively low.

The economy therefore experiences two simultaneous changes:

\[
L_S \uparrow\uparrow
\]

and:

\[
L_U \downarrow\downarrow
\]

This creates a **skills abundance / routine-labour scarcity** economy.

---

# 8. The First Consequence: Wage Compression at the Bottom

The most immediate consequence could be increasing wages for traditionally low-paid occupations.

Consider a simple example.

Before educational improvement:

| Occupation | Monthly Wage |
|---|---:|
| Cleaner | ₹12,000 |
| Farm worker | ₹13,000 |
| Delivery worker | ₹15,000 |
| Factory worker | ₹18,000 |
| Technician | ₹35,000 |
| Engineer | ₹70,000 |

After educational improvement:

| Occupation | Possible Wage |
|---|---:|
| Cleaner | ₹25,000 |
| Farm worker | ₹27,000 |
| Delivery worker | ₹30,000 |
| Factory worker | ₹35,000 |
| Technician | ₹55,000 |
| Engineer | ₹90,000 |

Why would cleaner wages rise?

Not because cleaning suddenly requires advanced mathematics.

Rather:

> **There are fewer people willing to perform cleaning at the old wage.**

The employer has to compete for workers.

This is a crucial distinction.

---

# 9. Cheap Labour Becomes Expensive Labour

The phrase "cheap labour" often hides an important economic assumption:

> There must be enough workers willing to work for low wages.

If that condition disappears, the business model changes.

Suppose a restaurant historically spends:

\[
₹100,000
\]

per month on kitchen and cleaning labour.

After widespread educational improvement, labour costs increase to:

\[
₹200,000
\]

The restaurant now has several choices.

### Option 1: Raise prices

A meal that cost ₹200 may become ₹230.

### Option 2: Reduce staffing

Instead of 10 employees, employ 7.

### Option 3: Automate

Install:

- automated dishwashers,
- robotic food preparation,
- ordering kiosks,
- inventory systems,
- automated cleaning systems.

### Option 4: Increase worker productivity

Give each employee better tools and systems.

### Option 5: Close

If the economics no longer work, the business may leave the market.

This is how labour scarcity can restructure an industry.

---

# 10. Automation Becomes Economically Attractive

This may be the most important consequence.

Suppose a machine costs:

\[
₹20,00,000
\]

and lasts for 10 years.

Ignoring financing and maintenance for simplicity:

\[
₹20,00,000 / 10
=
₹2,00,000
\]

per year.

If the machine replaces workers costing:

\[
₹6,00,000
\]

per year, automation becomes attractive.

But if labour costs only:

\[
₹1,50,000
\]

per year, automation may not make economic sense.

Therefore:

\[
\text{Higher Wages}
\rightarrow
\text{Higher Automation Incentive}
\]

This produces an important feedback loop:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Education}
\rightarrow
\text{Labour Scarcity}
\rightarrow
\text{Higher Wages}
\rightarrow
\text{Automation}
}
\]

---

# 11. The Paradoxical Result

The extremely good school may initially create a labour shortage.

But that shortage may encourage technological development.

Technology then reduces the need for the scarce labour.

For example:

\[
100 \text{ workers}
\rightarrow
20 \text{ workers + machines}
\]

Eventually:

\[
20 \text{ workers}
\rightarrow
5 \text{ highly skilled workers + advanced automation}
\]

The economy has transformed.

The original labour shortage becomes an incentive for productivity growth.

Thus:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Labour Shortage}
\rightarrow
\text{Innovation}
\rightarrow
\text{Productivity Growth}
}
\]

---

# 12. The Economy May Become Better

This is where the apparent "curse" becomes complicated.

Suppose an economy previously relied on:

\[
1,000
\]

workers to produce a certain amount of output.

After technological improvement, it needs:

\[
100
\]

workers.

The remaining 900 people can potentially work in other sectors.

They may create:

- new software,
- new medicines,
- new machines,
- new scientific discoveries,
- new businesses,
- new infrastructure,
- new entertainment,
- new financial services,
- new educational technologies.

The economy becomes more diversified.

Therefore, the disappearance of certain jobs does not necessarily mean the disappearance of economic activity.

---

# 13. Baumol's Cost Disease

One useful economic concept related to this discussion is **Baumol's cost disease**.

Some activities are difficult to automate or increase in productivity.

For example, suppose a classical live performance requires:

\[
50
\]

musicians.

Technological improvements might dramatically improve manufacturing productivity, but the performance may still require roughly the same number of human performers.

If manufacturing wages rise because productivity increases elsewhere, musicians' wages may also need to rise to compete for labour.

Therefore:

\[
\text{Productivity Growth in Some Sectors}
\rightarrow
\text{Wage Growth}
\rightarrow
\text{Higher Costs in Less-Automatable Sectors}
\]

This matters for the extremely well-educated society.

Some jobs are inherently human-intensive.

Examples may include:

- childcare,
- eldercare,
- certain healthcare services,
- personal services,
- teaching,
- hospitality,
- live performance,
- interpersonal care.

These sectors may become increasingly expensive.

---

# 14. The Price of Human Attention

An extremely educated society could also make human attention more valuable.

Imagine everyone can perform sophisticated technical tasks.

Human attention becomes scarce.

Consider:

- teachers,
- doctors,
- therapists,
- consultants,
- skilled mechanics,
- experienced engineers,
- researchers,
- managers.

Even if education becomes widespread, expertise may remain scarce at the highest levels.

The economy may therefore develop a hierarchy:

\[
\text{Basic Skills}
\rightarrow
\text{Advanced Skills}
\rightarrow
\text{Expert Skills}
\rightarrow
\text{Exceptional Expertise}
\]

Improving education shifts more people upward, but it does not necessarily eliminate scarcity.

---

# 15. Education Does Not Make Everyone Identical

Another important limitation is that education cannot perfectly equalize ability.

People differ in:

- interests,
- motivation,
- personality,
- creativity,
- physical abilities,
- learning speed,
- risk tolerance,
- preferences,
- experience,
- specialization.

Even an excellent school cannot make every student identical.

Instead, it raises the overall distribution.

Imagine this simplified distribution:

### Poor education

\[
\text{Low} \quad \text{Low} \quad \text{Medium} \quad \text{High}
\]

### Excellent education

\[
\text{Medium} \quad \text{High} \quad \text{High} \quad \text{Very High}
\]

The entire population becomes more capable, but differences remain.

Therefore, some people will still prefer or specialize in occupations that others do not.

---

# 16. Who Will Do the "Bad Jobs"?

This is one of the hardest questions created by the thought experiment.

If everyone is capable of becoming a programmer, engineer, scientist, entrepreneur, or doctor:

> Who cleans the buildings?

The answer cannot simply be:

> "Someone will do it."

If nobody wants to do it at the old wage, the market has to change.

There are several possible solutions.

---

# 17. Solution One: Higher Wages

The simplest solution is:

\[
\text{Bad Job}
+
\text{Higher Wage}
\rightarrow
\text{Workers}
\]

A difficult, unpleasant, dangerous, or boring job can become attractive if it pays sufficiently well.

Suppose cleaning pays:

\[
₹12,000
\]

but nobody wants to do it.

Raise it to:

\[
₹25,000
\]

Some workers may reconsider.

Raise it to:

\[
₹40,000
\]

Now the job may become economically competitive.

This demonstrates an important principle:

> **There is rarely a universal shortage of workers. There is often a shortage of workers at a particular wage and under particular conditions.**

---

# 18. Solution Two: Automation

The second solution is automation.

Instead of asking:

> "How do we convince people to clean?"

the economy asks:

> "How do we eliminate the need for humans to clean?"

Possible technologies include:

- robotic vacuum cleaners,
- autonomous floor scrubbers,
- robotic window cleaners,
- automated waste collection,
- machine-assisted sanitation,
- automated warehouses,
- autonomous agricultural machinery,
- robotic manufacturing.

The higher cost of human labour provides a financial incentive to develop these technologies.

---

# 19. Solution Three: Immigration

An economy experiencing labour shortages can sometimes attract workers from other regions.

Suppose Country A has:

\[
\text{Very High Education}
\]

and therefore:

\[
\text{Low Supply of Low-Wage Labour}
\]

Country B may have:

\[
\text{Lower Average Education}
\]

and therefore a larger supply of workers willing to perform lower-paid occupations.

Country A could attract workers from Country B.

This creates:

\[
\text{Labour Migration}
\rightarrow
\text{Increased Labour Supply}
\rightarrow
\text{Reduced Labour Shortage}
\]

However, immigration is a complex political, social, legal, and economic issue.

It cannot simply be assumed to solve every labour shortage.

---

# 20. Solution Four: Redesign the Job

Sometimes the answer is not higher wages or automation.

It is better job design.

Suppose a job originally requires:

- 8 hours of repetitive work,
- heavy paperwork,
- inefficient processes,
- poor scheduling.

A redesigned system might reduce it to:

- 4 hours,
- automated paperwork,
- better tools,
- more efficient organization.

Then fewer workers are required.

Thus:

\[
\text{Organizational Innovation}
\rightarrow
\text{Higher Labour Productivity}
\]

---

# 21. Solution Five: Make Low-Skill Work More Attractive

Not every worker wants a prestigious career.

Some people value:

- flexible schedules,
- proximity to home,
- low stress,
- independence,
- social interaction,
- physical activity,
- predictable hours.

A society could therefore improve undesirable occupations by making them:

- safer,
- more flexible,
- more autonomous,
- better paid,
- socially respected.

This can increase labour supply without requiring workers to become less educated.

---

# 22. The Role of Social Status

An overlooked component of labour markets is status.

People do not work only for money.

They also consider:

- prestige,
- identity,
- social recognition,
- purpose,
- autonomy,
- working environment.

If a society treats certain essential occupations as inferior, highly educated people may avoid them even when the wages are reasonable.

Therefore, an extremely educated society might need to rethink the social hierarchy attached to occupations.

A garbage collector performs an essential economic function.

A sanitation worker performs an essential public-health function.

A construction worker builds physical infrastructure.

A farmer produces food.

A cleaner maintains usable environments.

Their educational credentials do not determine the social value of the work.

---

# 23. The Difference Between Skill and Value

A highly skilled job is not necessarily more socially valuable than a low-skilled job.

For example:

\[
\text{Software Engineer}
\]

may have high skill requirements.

But:

\[
\text{Sanitation Worker}
\]

may perform a function essential to public health.

Economic wages are not perfect measures of social value.

They reflect market conditions, scarcity, productivity, bargaining power, institutions, and demand.

This distinction becomes increasingly important in a highly educated society.

---

# 24. What Happens to Businesses?

Businesses depending on cheap labour would face the greatest pressure.

Consider industries such as:

- agriculture,
- textiles,
- food service,
- construction,
- logistics,
- cleaning,
- warehousing,
- manufacturing,
- domestic services.

Their traditional model may be:

\[
\text{Low Wage}
+
\text{Many Workers}
\rightarrow
\text{Low Unit Cost}
\]

But after widespread education:

\[
\text{High Wage}
+
\text{Fewer Workers}
\rightarrow
\text{Higher Unit Cost}
\]

Businesses must therefore transform.

---

# 25. From Labour-Intensive to Capital-Intensive Production

An economy may shift from:

\[
\text{Labour Intensive}
\]

to:

\[
\text{Capital Intensive}
\]

A labour-intensive factory might require:

\[
500 \text{ workers}
\]

A highly automated factory might require:

\[
50 \text{ engineers and technicians}
\]

plus:

\[
\text{robots + computers + machinery}
\]

The composition of the economy changes.

Human labour does not disappear.

Its role changes.

---

# 26. Human Capital Becomes More Important

Human capital refers broadly to the knowledge, skills, abilities, and experience possessed by people.

An extremely effective school dramatically increases human capital.

Instead of viewing education as merely preparing students for existing jobs, we can view it as increasing the productive capacity of society.

The production function can be simplified as:

\[
Y = F(K,L,H,A)
\]

where:

- \(Y\) = output,
- \(K\) = physical capital,
- \(L\) = labour,
- \(H\) = human capital,
- \(A\) = technology/productivity.

Exceptional education increases:

\[
H
\]

But increasing \(H\) may also accelerate:

\[
A
\]

because highly educated workers are more capable of creating and implementing technology.

Thus:

\[
H \uparrow
\Rightarrow
A \uparrow
\]

can be a powerful feedback mechanism.

---

# 27. Education Can Create New Jobs

The argument that education eliminates low-skilled jobs can overlook another phenomenon:

> **New technologies create entirely new occupations.**

Consider occupations that were rare or nonexistent in earlier eras:

- software developer,
- cloud engineer,
- machine-learning engineer,
- cybersecurity analyst,
- data scientist,
- UX designer,
- mobile application developer,
- AI researcher,
- drone operator,
- renewable-energy engineer.

The economy continuously creates new categories of work.

Therefore, the disappearance of some jobs does not necessarily mean total employment collapses.

---

# 28. The Historical Pattern

Technological progress has repeatedly destroyed some occupations while creating others.

Examples include:

### Agriculture

Mechanization dramatically reduced the proportion of people required to work directly in agriculture.

Yet agricultural output increased enormously.

### Manufacturing

Automation reduced the number of workers required for many production processes.

But industrial productivity increased.

### Banking

Computers eliminated enormous amounts of manual bookkeeping.

But financial technology created new professions.

### Telecommunications

Older communication jobs disappeared.

New technology industries emerged.

The general pattern is:

\[
\text{Technology}
\rightarrow
\text{Job Destruction}
+
\text{Job Creation}
\]

The transition can nevertheless be painful.

---

# 29. The Real Problem: Transition Costs

Even if the long-term economy becomes richer, individual workers can suffer during transitions.

Suppose:

\[
100,000
\]

factory workers become unnecessary because of automation.

The economy may eventually create:

\[
80,000
\]

new technical jobs.

But those new jobs may require completely different skills.

The former factory workers cannot instantly become:

- robotics engineers,
- software developers,
- data scientists,
- electrical engineers.

Therefore:

\[
\text{Structural Change}
\rightarrow
\text{Skill Mismatch}
\]

This is one of the greatest challenges of technological transformation.

---

# 30. The Extremely Good School Could Reduce This Problem

Ironically, the same school that creates labour shortages could also make the transition easier.

If students develop:

- adaptability,
- mathematics,
- computing,
- communication,
- critical thinking,
- self-learning,
- technical literacy,

they may be better prepared for technological change.

The most valuable educational outcome may therefore not be memorization.

It may be:

\[
\boxed{\text{Ability to Learn}}
\]

A person who can learn new skills rapidly can adapt to changing labour markets.

---

# 31. Education as an Economic Accelerator

A powerful education system can create a feedback loop.

\[
\text{Better Education}
\]

leads to:

\[
\text{Higher Productivity}
\]

which leads to:

\[
\text{Higher Income}
\]

which leads to:

\[
\text{More Investment}
\]

which leads to:

\[
\text{Better Technology}
\]

which leads to:

\[
\text{Higher Productivity}
\]

and the cycle continues.

Symbolically:

\[
E \rightarrow P \rightarrow I \rightarrow T \rightarrow P
\]

where:

- \(E\) = education,
- \(P\) = productivity,
- \(I\) = investment,
- \(T\) = technology.

Thus, the "curse" may actually become an **economic accelerator**.

---

# 32. The Cheap-Labour Economy

Many developing economies have historically benefited from large supplies of relatively inexpensive labour.

The basic development model can look like:

\[
\text{Large Labour Supply}
+
\text{Low Wages}
+
\text{Foreign Investment}
\rightarrow
\text{Industrialization}
\]

Factories move toward locations where production costs are lower.

But if education improves dramatically and wages rise:

\[
\text{Low-Wage Advantage}
\downarrow
\]

The country may lose some labour-intensive industries.

This could initially appear negative.

However, it can encourage movement toward higher-value industries.

# 34. The "Curse" May Actually Be a Development Threshold

Therefore, what initially appears to be a curse may actually indicate that an economy has reached a particular developmental threshold.

The country effectively says:

> "We can no longer compete primarily by paying people very little."

It must instead compete through:

- productivity,
- technology,
- infrastructure,
- innovation,
- quality,
- specialization,
- intellectual property,
- advanced manufacturing.

This can be uncomfortable, but it can also be economically transformative.

# 35. The Restaurant Example

Consider a restaurant.

### Old economy

Employees:

- 2 cooks
- 2 cleaners
- 2 servers
- 1 cashier

Total:

\[
7 \text{ workers}
\]

Suppose average monthly labour cost is:

\[
₹15,000
\]

Total:

\[
₹105,000
\]

### Highly educated economy

Few people want low-paid repetitive jobs.

Average labour cost becomes:

\[

# 43. The Productivity Escape

# 57. A More Formal Model

Let:

\[
N
\]

be the total population.

Let:

\[
p
\]

be the proportion willing and qualified to perform low-skill work.

Then:

\[
L_U = Np
\]

If education reduces \(p\):

\[
\frac{dp}{dE}<0
\]

where \(E\) represents educational effectiveness.

Then:

\[
\frac{dL_U}{dE}
=
N\frac{dp}{dE}
<0
\]

Thus, under the model:

> Increasing educational effectiveness decreases the supply of workers willing or qualified to perform low-skill jobs.

If labour demand \(D_U\) remains constant, equilibrium wages tend to rise.

Symbolically:

\[
W_U = f(D_U,L_U)
\]

with:

\[
\frac{\partial W_U}{\partial L_U}<0
\]

Therefore:

\[
L_U\downarrow
\Rightarrow
W_U\uparrow
\]

---

# 58. Introducing Automation

Let \(A\) represent automation.

Suppose automation is positively related to labour costs:

\[
A=f(W_U)
\]

with:

\[
\frac{dA}{dW_U}>0
\]

Then:

\[
E\uparrow
\rightarrow
L_U\downarrow
\rightarrow
W_U\uparrow
\rightarrow
A\uparrow
\]

Automation then reduces the required amount of routine labour:

\[
\frac{\partial D_U}{\partial A}<0
\]

Thus:

\[
A\uparrow
\rightarrow
D_U\downarrow
\]

The system eventually moves toward a new equilibrium.

---

# 59. A Full Feedback Loop

The complete conceptual system can therefore be represented as:

\[
\boxed{
E
\uparrow
\rightarrow
H
\uparrow
\rightarrow
L_U
\downarrow
\rightarrow
W_U
\uparrow
\rightarrow
A
\uparrow
\rightarrow
D_U
\downarrow
\rightarrow
\text{Productivity}
\uparrow
}
\]

where:

- \(E\) = educational effectiveness,
- \(H\) = human capital,
- \(L_U\) = routine labour supply,
- \(W_U\) = routine-labour wage,
- \(A\) = automation,
- \(D_U\) = demand for routine labour.

This is the central mechanism of the thought experiment.

# 74. The Ultimate Productivity Question

The central economic question becomes:

# 88. The School's Actual "Curse"

If we insist on keeping the metaphor, the real curse is not:

> "Everyone is too smart."

It is:

> **"The economic system must evolve when its people become too productive to fit comfortably into its old labour structure."**

The school has not failed.

The old economic model has become obsolete.

# 89. A Broader Principle

The thought experiment reveals a broader principle of economic development:

}

If the economy fails to reorganize, unemployment, labour shortages, skill mismatch, and inequality can appear.

If it successfully reorganizes, society can experience:

- higher productivity,
- higher wages,
- better technology,

- improved living standards.

# 90. Conclusion

The idea of the **"Curse of the Extremely Good School"** begins with an apparently strange proposition:

> What if a school became so good that almost everyone who attended it became highly skilled?

The immediate economic consequence could indeed be a shortage of cheap labour.

Workers would have better alternatives.

Low-paid occupations would become harder to fill.

Wages would rise.

Labour-intensive businesses would face higher costs.

Some cheap services would become more expensive.

Companies would be forced to respond.

But this is only the first stage.

Higher labour costs create incentives for:

- automation,
- robotics,
- AI,
- better job design,
- capital investment,
- technological innovation,
- productivity improvements.

As productivity rises, the economy can produce more with fewer workers.

The resulting economy may therefore move away from:

\[
\text{Cheap Labour}
\]

toward:

\[
\text{High Human Capital}
+
\text{Advanced Technology}
\]

This transformation can be painful, especially for workers and industries caught between old and new economic systems. It can also produce inequality if the gains from automation and technological progress are concentrated among a small group of capital owners.

Nevertheless, the disappearance of cheap labour should not automatically be interpreted as economic failure.

In many cases, it may represent something more fundamental:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Society Has Become Too Productive for Its Old Economic Structure}
}
\]

The real challenge is therefore not to preserve cheap labour forever.

The challenge is to build an economic system capable of **using highly educated humans effectively**.

A society should not deliberately keep people undereducated simply because an old economic model depends upon cheap labour.

Instead, it should allow education to raise human capability and then allow technology, markets, institutions, and economic policy to adapt.

The ultimate objective should be:

\[
\boxed{
\text{More Capable Humans}
+
\text{More Capable Machines}
=
\text{More Productive Society}
}
\]

The apparent curse can therefore become a catalyst.

The school does not destroy the economy.

It destroys the **need for the economy to depend on cheap human labour**.

And that distinction is enormous.

---

# 91. Final Thesis

The entire thought experiment can be compressed into one chain:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Exceptional Education}
\rightarrow
\text{Universal Human Capital}
\rightarrow
\text{Scarcity of Cheap Labour}
\rightarrow
\text{Higher Wages}
\rightarrow
\text{Automation}
\rightarrow
\text{Higher Productivity}
\rightarrow
\text{Economic Transformation}
}
\]

Therefore:

> **The "Curse of the Extremely Good School" is not fundamentally the destruction of the economy by education. It is the economic pressure created when education succeeds so dramatically that an economy built around cheap human labour can no longer remain unchanged.**

In the short term, this may look like a labour shortage.

In the medium term, it may look like rising prices and automation.

In the long term, it may become a transition toward a high-wage, high-productivity, technologically advanced economy.

The paradox is therefore profound:

\[
\boxed{
\text{The better humans become at creating value, the less economically rational it becomes to use humans for tasks that machines can perform.}
}
\]

And that may be the ultimate "curse" of an extraordinarily successful education system:

> **It forces society to become worthy of the people it has educated.**
