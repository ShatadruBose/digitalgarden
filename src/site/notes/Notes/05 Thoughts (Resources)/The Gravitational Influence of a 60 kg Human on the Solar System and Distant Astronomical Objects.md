# The Gravitational Influence of a 60 kg Human on the Solar System and Distant Astronomical Objects

## An Extensive Quantitative Study of How Much Gravitational Force One Human Exerts on the Universe

---

## Abstract

Every object possessing mass participates in gravity.

A human being does not merely experience gravity from the Earth, Sun, Moon, planets, stars, and galaxies. The human also gravitationally attracts every one of those objects. This is a direct consequence of Newton's law of universal gravitation and, more fundamentally, of general relativity.

This paper examines a standardized thought experiment:

> A human with a mass of 60 kg is located on Earth, and the gravitational force that this human exerts on various astronomical objects is calculated.

The objects considered are:

- the Sun
- Mercury
- Venus
- the Moon
- Mars
- Jupiter
- Saturn
- Uranus
- Neptune
- Pluto
- Sagittarius A*
- the Andromeda Galaxy
- TON 618

The central equation is Newton's law of universal gravitation:

\[
F=\frac{GMm}{r^2}
\]

where:

- \(F\) is gravitational force,
- \(G\) is the gravitational constant,
- \(M\) is the mass of the astronomical object,
- \(m=60\,\mathrm{kg}\) is the human's mass,
- \(r\) is the separation between the human and the astronomical object.

The calculations demonstrate an extraordinary fact:

> Even a 60 kg human exerts a nonzero gravitational force on objects billions of light-years away.

That force becomes extraordinarily small with distance, but it does not become mathematically zero.

For example, under the standardized assumptions used in this paper:

\[
F_{\text{human}\rightarrow\text{Sun}}\approx3.56\times10^{-1}\,\mathrm{N}
\]

while

\[
F_{\text{human}\rightarrow\text{TON 618}}
\approx1.09\times10^{-20}\,\mathrm{N}.
\]

The gravitational interaction is reciprocal. If the human exerts a force \(F\) on an object, the object exerts the same magnitude of force \(F\) on the human, in the opposite direction, in accordance with Newton's third law.

The difference is not force but acceleration. A massive object such as the Sun, Jupiter, Sagittarius A*, or a galaxy changes its motion by an utterly negligible amount in response to one 60 kg human.

This paper develops the mathematics, assumptions, limitations, numerical results, physical interpretation, and deeper implications of this idea.

# 1. Introduction

Gravity is often taught from the human perspective.

We say:

- Earth pulls us downward.
- The Moon pulls the oceans.
- The Sun holds the planets in orbit.
- Jupiter has a strong gravitational field.
- Black holes have extreme gravity.

All of these statements are correct.

However, they can create an incomplete intuition.

A human is not merely a passive recipient of gravitational forces.

The human also creates a gravitational field.

A 60 kg human attracts:

- the floor,
- the Earth,
- the Moon,
- the Sun,
- every planet,
- every star,
- the center of the Milky Way,
- the Andromeda Galaxy,
- and even extraordinarily distant quasars such as TON 618.

The magnitude of this attraction varies enormously.

The interaction with Earth is strong on everyday scales.

The interaction with the Moon is much weaker.

The interaction with the Sun is smaller still.

The interaction with distant galaxies can be incredibly tiny.

The interaction with objects billions of light-years away is so small that it is practically irrelevant to ordinary engineering or human-scale physics.

But "practically irrelevant" does not mean "zero."

This distinction is one of the most interesting aspects of gravitational physics.

# 2. The Human's 60 kg Mass

## 2.1 Mass versus weight

A crucial terminology distinction should be made at the beginning.

A person does not technically have a "weight of 60 kg."

Kilograms measure **mass**.

Weight is a force and is measured in newtons.

Therefore, the physically correct statement is:

> The human has a mass of 60 kg.

Near Earth's surface, the person's weight is approximately:

\[
W=mg
\]

Using:

\[
m=60\,\mathrm{kg}
\]

and

\[
g\approx9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2},
\]

we obtain:

\[
W=60(9.81)
\]

\[
W\approx588.6\,\mathrm{N}.
\]

Thus:

\[
\boxed{W\approx589\,\mathrm{N}}
\]

The 60 kg mass is the quantity used in the gravitational-force calculations throughout this paper.

---

# 3. Newton's Law of Universal Gravitation

The fundamental equation is:

\[
\boxed{F=\frac{GMm}{r^2}}
\]

where:

\[
G=6.67430\times10^{-11}\,\mathrm{N\,m^2/kg^2}
\]

is the gravitational constant.

For this paper:

\[
m=60\,\mathrm{kg}.
\]

Therefore:

\[
F=\frac{G M(60)}{r^2}.
\]

This equation immediately reveals three important properties.

## 3.1 Force is proportional to the human's mass

If the person's mass doubles:

\[
m\rightarrow2m,
\]

then:

\[
F\rightarrow2F.
\]

A 120 kg person would therefore exert twice the gravitational force of a 60 kg person at the same distance from the same object.

---

## 3.2 Force is proportional to the astronomical object's mass

If the target object's mass doubles:

\[
M\rightarrow2M,
\]

then:

\[
F\rightarrow2F.
\]

This is why massive bodies such as the Sun and Jupiter can exert substantial gravitational forces despite large distances.

---

## 3.3 Force decreases with the square of distance

The inverse-square relationship is:

\[
F\propto\frac{1}{r^2}.
\]

This is extremely important.

If the distance doubles:

\[
r\rightarrow2r,
\]

then:

\[
F\rightarrow\frac{F}{4}.
\]

If the distance becomes ten times larger:

\[
r\rightarrow10r,
\]

then:

\[
F\rightarrow\frac{F}{100}.
\]

If the distance becomes one million times larger:

\[
r\rightarrow10^6r,
\]

then:

\[
F\rightarrow10^{-12}F.
\]

Distance is therefore devastating to gravitational interaction strength.

---

# 4. Newton's Third Law

There is a second fundamental principle involved.

Newton's third law states that forces between interacting bodies occur in equal and opposite pairs.

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{F_{A\rightarrow B}=-F_{B\rightarrow A}}
\]

in vector form.

In terms of magnitudes:

\[
\boxed{|F_{A\rightarrow B}|=|F_{B\rightarrow A}|}
\]

Thus, if a 60 kg human exerts:

\[
0.356\,\mathrm{N}
\]

on the Sun, then the Sun exerts:

\[
0.356\,\mathrm{N}
\]

on the human.

The human does not exert a weaker force merely because they are less massive.

The force is equal.

What differs dramatically is acceleration.

---

# 5. Force versus Acceleration

Newton's second law gives:

\[
F=ma.
\]

Therefore:

\[
a=\frac{F}{m}.
\]

Suppose a human and Jupiter exert a force \(F\) on each other.

For the human:

\[
a_{\text{human}}=\frac{F}{60}.
\]

For Jupiter:

\[
a_{\text{Jupiter}}=\frac{F}{M_J}.
\]

Since:

\[
M_J\gg60\,\mathrm{kg},
\]

we have:

\[
a_{\text{Jupiter}}\ll a_{\text{human}}.
\]

This is why Jupiter does not visibly move toward a single human despite the gravitational interaction being perfectly real.

The gravitational force is symmetric.

The resulting acceleration is not.

---

# 6. What Does "Located on Earth" Mean?

The standardized condition for this paper is:

> The 60 kg human remains on or within the Earth, and all target-object forces are calculated relative to Earth's location.

This eliminates a common ambiguity.

For example, when discussing Jupiter, one could ask:

- What force acts on the human when the human is near Jupiter?
- What force acts on the human when the human is at Earth?
- What force would exist if the human travelled to Jupiter?

Those are three different problems.

This paper uses the second:

\[
\boxed{\text{Human location = Earth}}
\]

The human is not transported around the Solar System.

# 7. Important Distance Assumptions

A real Solar System calculation is time-dependent.

Earth and every planet continuously move.

Therefore there is no single permanent distance between Earth and:

- Mercury,
- Venus,
- Mars,
- Jupiter,
- Saturn,
- Uranus,
- Neptune,
- Pluto.

For that reason, representative distances are used for the planetary examples.

The numbers in this paper should therefore be understood as **illustrative standardized estimates**, not instantaneous ephemeris calculations for a particular second.

For the Moon:

\[
r\approx384,400\,\mathrm{km}.
\]

For the Sun:

\[
r\approx1\,\mathrm{AU}.
\]

For several planets, representative Earth-object separations are adopted for comparison.

For extremely distant objects, astronomical distances are used.

NASA gives the distance to the Galactic Center/Sagittarius region as about:

\[
26,000\text{ light-years}.
\]

NASA gives the distance to Andromeda as approximately:

\[
2.5\text{ million light-years}.
\]

TON 618 is substantially farther away, and its distance depends on which cosmological distance definition is used. One commonly cited present comoving distance is approximately:

\[
18.2\text{ billion light-years}.
\]

Because cosmological distance is subtle, the TON 618 result should be treated as an order-of-magnitude calculation rather than a precision laboratory measurement.

# 10. The Sun

The Sun has a mass of approximately:

\[
M_\odot=1.98847\times10^{30}\,\mathrm{kg}.
\]

Using:

\[
r=1\,\mathrm{AU}
\]

and:

\[
m=60\,\mathrm{kg},
\]

we obtain:

\[
F=\frac{(6.67430\times10^{-11})
(1.98847\times10^{30})(60)}
{(1.495978707\times10^{11})^2}.
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{F\approx0.356\,\mathrm{N}}
\]

So a 60 kg human located on Earth exerts approximately:

\[
\boxed{0.36\,\mathrm{N}}
\]

of gravitational force on the Sun under the 1 AU approximation.

And the Sun exerts the same magnitude of force on the human.

That is a remarkable result.

A human's gravitational pull on the Sun is not zero.

It is approximately one-third of a newton.

However, relative to the Earth's pull on the human:

\[
F_{\text{Earth}}\approx589\,\mathrm{N},
\]

the Sun's force is tiny.

The ratio is approximately:

\[
\frac{0.356}{588.6}\approx6.05\times10^{-4}.
\]

Thus the Sun's gravitational force on the person is roughly:

\[
0.0605\%
\]

of the person's Earth-surface weight under this simplified comparison.

# 24. Why Does the Human Affect Anything at All?

A fundamental question follows:

> Why should a 60 kg human affect the Sun or a distant galaxy at all?

Because gravity is not a threshold phenomenon.

There is no rule saying:

> "Objects below a certain mass do not gravitate."

Instead:

\[
F\propto Mm.
\]

Any nonzero mass participates in gravitational interaction.

As long as:

\[
M>0
\]

and:

\[
m>0,
\]

and the separation is finite:

\[
r<\infty,
\]

Newtonian gravity predicts:

\[
F>0.
\]

The force may be tiny.

It may be negligible.

It may be impossible to measure in practice.

But it is not zero in the idealized model.

# 28. Why Jupiter Is Not Automatically the Largest

Someone might reason:

> Jupiter is the largest planet, so surely Jupiter must exert the greatest gravitational force.

That is incomplete.

The equation is:

\[
F=\frac{GMm}{r^2}.
\]

Jupiter has a tremendous mass.

But the Sun is vastly more massive:

\[
M_\odot\approx1.99\times10^{30}\,\mathrm{kg}
\]

while Jupiter has:

\[
M_J\approx1.90\times10^{27}\,\mathrm{kg}.
\]

The Sun is approximately:

\[
\frac{1.99\times10^{30}}

# 34. The Infinite-Distance Limit

So "extremely tiny" and "exactly zero" are mathematically different concepts.

# 37. Why We Normally Ignore a Human's Gravity

# 38. Newtonian Gravity Is an Approximation

is a simplified Newtonian interpretation.

The more fundamental statement is that the human contributes an extraordinarily tiny amount of stress-energy to spacetime.

That contribution is so small that the Newtonian description is entirely adequate for this thought experiment.

# 40. What Happens to Earth Because of the Human?

The human therefore causes an unimaginably tiny displacement of Earth's motion.

This is real two-body mechanics.

# 49. A Deeper General-Relativistic Caveat

At enormous cosmological distances, the universe is expanding.

The real universe is described, on large scales, by relativistic cosmology.

Therefore a calculation such as:

\[
F=\frac{GMm}{r^2}
\]

for TON 618 is best described as:

That distinction is essential.

# 51. The Finite-Speed Issue

For a static thought experiment, however, the Newtonian approximation is acceptable for demonstrating the underlying relationship between mass, distance, and gravitational interaction.

# 65. A Human Inside Earth

The original wording "I am in the Earth" can mean literally inside Earth.

Inside a spherical body, the gravitational field is more subtle.

- mantle,
- outer core,
- inner core.

# 66. The Shell Theorem

Newton's shell theorem states that for a spherically symmetric shell:

- outside the shell, the gravitational field behaves as though all mass were concentrated at the center;
- inside the shell, the net gravitational field is zero.

This is why:

\[
F=\frac{GMm}{r^2}
\]

works so cleanly for an ideal spherical planet when the observer is outside the planet.

This simplification is extremely useful in planetary mechanics.

# 67. Why Mass Distribution Matters for Galaxies

For galaxies the point-mass approximation is less exact.

Its mass is distributed across a vast region.

Therefore, a simplistic calculation such as:

\[
F=\frac{GMm}{r^2}
\]

using one total galaxy mass is useful for intuition, but not sufficient for detailed galactic dynamics.

# 68. A More Complete Galactic Description

In general, the gravitational field is derived from the mass density:

\[
\rho(\mathbf r).
\]

The gravitational potential in Newtonian theory obeys Poisson's equation:

\[
\nabla^2\Phi=4\pi G\rho.
\]

The gravitational acceleration is then:

\[
\mathbf g=-\nabla\Phi.
\]

For an extended galaxy, one can write the potential contribution schematically as:

\[
\Phi(\mathbf x)
=
-G
\int
\frac{\rho(\mathbf x')}{|\mathbf x-\mathbf x'|}
\,d^3x'.
\]

This is far more realistic than pretending the entire galaxy is one point.

Here:

A human possesses stress-energy.

\boxed{\text{A human is not gravitationally invisible.}}

# 91. The Test-Mass Idea
