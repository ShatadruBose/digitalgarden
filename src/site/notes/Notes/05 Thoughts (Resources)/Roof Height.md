# Roof Height: A Proposed Concept of a Universal Vertical Boundary

## Abstract

**Roof Height** is a conceptual term invented by the author to describe a hypothetical vertical boundary in an environment where every physical structure or natural object—such as buildings, trees, towers, mountains, poles, and other upward-extending features—reaches a definite maximum vertical level, while the entire region above that level is completely empty, flat, and free of physical objects.

In this concept, the **roof** does not necessarily refer to the literal roof of a building. Instead, it represents an abstract upper boundary of the occupied vertical world. Below the Roof Height exists the complete physical environment: architecture, vegetation, infrastructure, terrain, atmospheric space, and other objects. At the Roof Height, vertical extension effectively terminates. Above it lies an idealized empty region containing no buildings, trees, towers, mountains, clouds, or other structures.

The concept can be understood as a thought experiment involving **vertical limits, spatial occupancy, artificial boundaries, environmental geometry, urban morphology, and the relationship between physical objects and empty space**.

---

# 1. Introduction

Human environments are normally understood horizontally.

We describe places using streets, roads, boundaries, districts, cities, countries, and coordinates. Maps generally emphasize two-dimensional position: north–south and east–west. Even when elevation is considered, vertical space is usually treated as something continuous and effectively unlimited for ordinary human purposes.

The concept of **Roof Height** introduces a different way of thinking.

Instead of asking:

> "How high does a particular building, tree, mountain, or tower reach?"

the concept asks:

> "What is the highest vertical level occupied by anything in the entire environment?"

This produces a universal vertical boundary.

Suppose a hypothetical world contains:

- houses,
- skyscrapers,
- trees,
- communication towers,
- electricity pylons,
- mountains,
- hills,
- bridges,
- poles,
- monuments,
- cranes,
- and every other physical object.

Now imagine that all of these objects stop before reaching one particular elevation.

At that elevation, the physical world reaches its maximum vertical extent.

Everything above that level is completely empty and perfectly flat.

That boundary is called the **Roof Height**.

Roof Height therefore transforms the ordinary three-dimensional environment into a conceptual space consisting of two principal regions:

1. **The occupied vertical domain** — everything at or below the Roof Height.
2. **The empty upper domain** — the perfectly vacant region above the Roof Height.

The idea is deliberately idealized. It is not intended to describe the actual atmosphere or the real geometry of Earth. Rather, it provides a framework for imagining a world with a universal upper limit.

---

# 2. Definition of Roof Height

## 2.1 Basic Definition

**Roof Height** is defined as:

> The maximum vertical level of a specified environment at which all buildings, trees, structures, and other considered vertical objects terminate, with the region above that level being completely empty and flat.

The important word is **specified**.

Roof Height cannot be meaningfully defined without first defining the area or system being considered.

For example, one could define:

- a house's Roof Height,
- a school campus's Roof Height,
- a neighborhood's Roof Height,
- a city's Roof Height,
- a country's Roof Height,
- a continent's Roof Height,
- or an entire hypothetical planet's Roof Height.

Each would potentially have a different value.

---

# 3. The Fundamental Principle

The central rule of the concept can be expressed simply:

> **Nothing considered within the defined environment may extend above the Roof Height.**

If the Roof Height is:

\[
H_R = 100\text{ m}
\]

then every relevant object must satisfy:

\[
H_i \leq 100\text{ m}
\]

where \(H_i\) is the vertical height of object \(i\).

An object that attempts to extend above 100 m would violate the definition of the environment.

Therefore, the Roof Height acts as an **upper bound**.

Mathematically:

\[
H_R = \max(H_1,H_2,H_3,\ldots,H_n)
\]

where the \(H_i\) values represent the heights of all relevant objects.

However, the conceptual version goes further.

The statement is not merely:

> "100 m happens to be the highest point."

Instead, it establishes:

> "100 m is the universal termination level of the occupied vertical world."

This distinction is fundamental.

---

# 4. Roof Height Is Not Simply Building Height

A major source of confusion would be interpreting Roof Height as the height of a building.

It is not necessarily the same thing.

A building may have a height of:

\[
H_B = 30\text{ m}
\]

while the Roof Height of the surrounding environment may be:

\[
H_R = 100\text{ m}
\]

The building occupies only part of the available vertical domain.

Similarly, a tree could reach 60 m, a tower could reach 90 m, and another structure could reach 100 m.

The Roof Height represents the **maximum environmental vertical extent**, not the height of every object.

Thus:

\[
H_B < H_T < H_R
\]

could describe a building, tree, and Roof Height respectively.

---

# 5. The Visual Model

Imagine standing in a city governed by Roof Height.

Buildings of different sizes rise from the ground.

Trees rise between them.

Towers rise above some buildings.

Other structures occupy intermediate levels.

But eventually, every object reaches the same maximum boundary.

Above that boundary is nothing.

No:

- skyscrapers,
- mountains,
- trees,
- antennas,
- cranes,
- balloons,
- clouds,
- aircraft,
- or other considered objects.

The result would resemble an enormous artificial ceiling separating the occupied world from a perfectly empty upper space.

However, the Roof Height does not necessarily need to be physically solid.

It could be an **abstract mathematical boundary**.

---

# 6. Roof Height as a Geometric Concept

In three-dimensional geometry, an environment can be represented by coordinates:

\[
(x,y,z)
\]

where:

- \(x\) represents one horizontal dimension,
- \(y\) represents the other horizontal dimension,
- \(z\) represents vertical position.

Under the Roof Height concept:

\[
z \leq H_R
\]

for all occupied points.

The region above the boundary is:

\[
z > H_R
\]

and contains no objects.

Thus the Roof Height creates a horizontal boundary:

\[
z = H_R
\]

This is analogous to a mathematical plane.

The plane separates:

\[
z \leq H_R
\]

from:

\[
z > H_R
\]

The lower region contains the environment.

The upper region is empty.

---

# 7. Flatness

The concept specifically includes the idea that the upper boundary is **flat**.

This means that the Roof Height is constant across the defined region.

For example:

\[
H_R(x,y)=100\text{ m}
\]

for every position \((x,y)\).

Thus:

\[
H_R(0,0)=100\text{ m}
\]

\[
H_R(1000,500)=100\text{ m}
\]

\[
H_R(-200,900)=100\text{ m}
\]

and so forth.

The boundary does not rise or fall.

It does not follow hills.

It does not follow mountains.

It does not curve around skyscrapers.

It remains geometrically flat.

---

# 8. The Difference Between Ground Height and Roof Height

Roof Height should be distinguished from ground elevation.

Consider two locations.

Location A may have ground elevation:

\[
G_A=0\text{ m}
\]

while Location B has:

\[
G_B=30\text{ m}
\]

If the Roof Height is an absolute elevation of 100 m, then the available vertical space differs.

At A:

\[
100-0=100\text{ m}
\]

At B:

\[
100-30=70\text{ m}
\]

Therefore, the concept requires careful definition of the reference frame.

There are two major interpretations.

### Absolute Roof Height

The Roof Height is measured from a common reference plane.

\[
z=H_R
\]

### Local Roof Height

Every location has the same vertical distance from its own ground.

\[
z-G(x,y)=H_R
\]

These produce very different geometries.

---

# 9. Absolute Roof Height

Under the absolute model, the Roof Height is a universal horizontal plane.

For example:

\[
H_R=500\text{ m}
\]

means the entire environment terminates at elevation 500 m relative to the chosen reference level.

This produces a true flat upper boundary.

However, mountains or elevated terrain could consume much of the available vertical space.

A mountain with an elevation of 450 m would have only:

\[
500-450=50\text{ m}
\]

of remaining vertical space.

---

# 10. Local Roof Height

Under the local model, every location has its own vertical ceiling.

If the Roof Height is 100 m above ground:

\[
H_R(x,y)=G(x,y)+100
\]

This allows the boundary to follow terrain.

But this contradicts the strongest interpretation of the phrase **"fully empty flat"** if flatness is meant in the global geometric sense.

Therefore, for the purest version of the concept, **absolute Roof Height** is preferable.

---

# 11. Objects Under the Roof

The world below Roof Height can contain an enormous variety of objects.

These can be divided into several categories.

## 11.1 Buildings

Buildings include:

- houses,
- apartments,
- schools,
- hospitals,
- offices,
- factories,
- warehouses,
- skyscrapers,
- temples,
- churches,
- mosques,
- monuments,
- stadiums,
- shopping centers.

Every building must satisfy:

\[
H_{\text{building}}\leq H_R
\]

---

# 12. Trees

Trees are included because they are natural vertical structures.

A tree may have:

\[
H_{\text{tree}}=20\text{ m}
\]

while another may reach:

\[
H_{\text{tree}}=80\text{ m}
\]

If:

\[
H_R=100\text{ m}
\]

both are permitted.

A hypothetical 120 m tree would not be permitted because:

\[
120>100
\]

---

# 13. Towers

Towers are particularly important because they are designed specifically for vertical extension.

Examples include:

- radio towers,
- telecommunications towers,
- observation towers,
- transmission towers,
- clock towers,
- water towers,
- scientific towers.

The Roof Height therefore imposes a hard upper constraint on tower engineering.

A tower designed to reach 150 m would have to be redesigned if:

\[
H_R=100\text{ m}
\]

---

# 14. Antennas and Vertical Extensions

An important question arises:

Does the Roof Height include antennas?

Under the strict definition, yes.

If a building is 95 m tall but its antenna extends to 105 m, then the relevant vertical object reaches 105 m.

Therefore, if:

\[
H_R=100\text{ m}
\]

the antenna would violate the boundary.

This illustrates why Roof Height must define what counts as an object.

---

# 15. Natural Terrain

Mountains create an interesting problem.

Suppose the Roof Height is 1,000 m above a reference datum.

A mountain reaching 900 m occupies:

\[
900\text{ m}
\]

of vertical space.

Only:

\[
100\text{ m}
\]

remains above it.

A mountain reaching exactly 1,000 m touches the Roof Height.

A mountain that would naturally reach 1,200 m cannot exist under the strict model.

Therefore, the concept can be used to imagine a world in which even geology is vertically constrained.

---

# 16. The Roof Height Boundary

The boundary can be represented as:

\[
z=H_R
\]

Everything below it belongs to the occupied domain.

Everything above it belongs to the empty domain.

Thus:

\[
\mathcal{E}=\{(x,y,z):z\leq H_R\}
\]

and:

\[
\mathcal{V}=\{(x,y,z):z>H_R\}
\]

where:

- \(\mathcal{E}\) represents the permitted environmental domain,
- \(\mathcal{V}\) represents the empty vertical domain.

The Roof Height is therefore a **domain boundary**.

---

# 17. Roof Height and Empty Space

One of the most interesting aspects of the concept is that the Roof Height is defined not only by what exists below it, but also by what does **not** exist above it.

The upper region is completely empty.

This means that, under the idealized definition, there are no physical structures in that region.

The emptiness is absolute within the rules of the thought experiment.

This makes Roof Height fundamentally different from ordinary architectural roofs.

A building roof separates interior and exterior.

Roof Height separates:

> **occupied physical space from idealized empty vertical space.**

---

# 18. Why Call It a "Roof"?

The word "roof" is useful because a roof normally represents an upper boundary.

A house has:

- a floor,
- walls,
- and a roof.

The proposed concept extends this metaphor to the entire environment.

Instead of a house having a roof, the entire physical world under consideration has a conceptual roof.

Therefore:

**Roof + Height = the height of the universal environmental roof.**

The term does not require the boundary to be a literal material surface.

---

# 19. Roof Height Versus Ceiling

A ceiling and Roof Height are related but not identical.

A ceiling is generally an interior architectural surface.

Roof Height is an environmental limit.

A ceiling might be:

\[
3\text{ m}
\]

above a room's floor.

A Roof Height might be:

\[
100\text{ m}
\]

above the defined reference level.

Thus:

\[
H_{\text{ceiling}}\neq H_R
\]

in general.

---

# 20. Roof Height Versus Skyline

The skyline is the visible outline formed by structures and terrain against the sky.

Roof Height is different.

The skyline can vary continuously:

\[
S(x,y)
\]

while Roof Height can remain constant:

\[
H_R=\text{constant}
\]

Thus the skyline may look irregular while the Roof Height remains perfectly flat.

For example:

```text
Roof Height  ───────────────────────────────

             /\       /\       |
        /\  /  \     /  \      |
       /  \/    \   /    \     |
______/__________\_/______\____|___________
```

The skyline varies.

The Roof Height does not.

---

# 21. A World With Roof Height

Consider a hypothetical city with:

\[
H_R=200\text{ m}
\]

Its tallest structures may include:

- a 190 m tower,
- a 180 m skyscraper,
- a 150 m tree,
- a 120 m monument,
- several 100 m buildings.

None can exceed 200 m.

Above 200 m:

\[
z>200
\]

the environment is completely empty.

The city therefore has a universal vertical maximum.

---

# 22. Contact With the Roof Height

Objects can have different relationships with the boundary.

### Below the boundary

\[
H_i<H_R
\]

The object does not touch the Roof Height.

### Exactly touching the boundary

\[
H_i=H_R
\]

The object reaches the Roof Height.

### Above the boundary

\[
H_i>H_R
\]

The object violates the system.

Under a strict Roof Height world, the third case is impossible.

---

# 23. The Tallest Object

The tallest object is especially important.

Suppose the object heights are:

\[
10,\ 20,\ 35,\ 48,\ 75,\ 91,\ 99,\ 100
\]

Then:

\[
H_R=100
\]

if the Roof Height is defined as the maximum occupied height.

The 100 m object becomes the **Roof-Touching Object**.

It physically reaches the boundary.

This object could be:

- a tree,
- a building,
- a tower,
- a mountain,
- or another structure.

---

# 24. Multiple Roof-Touching Objects

There does not have to be only one object touching the Roof Height.

Suppose:

- Building A = 100 m
- Building B = 100 m
- Tree C = 100 m
- Tower D = 100 m

Then all four touch the Roof Height.

Therefore:

\[
\{i:H_i=H_R\}
\]

may contain many objects.

The boundary belongs to the environment rather than to one particular structure.

---

# 25. Roof Height as a Maximum Function

The simplest mathematical definition is:

\[
H_R=\max_i H_i
\]

This is useful for a finite collection of objects.

If there are infinitely many objects, the mathematical situation becomes more interesting.

There may be a supremum without an actual maximum.

For example:

\[
H_i=100-\frac{1}{i}
\]

gives:

\[
99,\ 99.5,\ 99.667,\ldots
\]

approaching 100 m.

The objects never actually reach 100 m.

Then:

\[
\sup H_i=100\text{ m}
\]

but there is no object with:

\[
H_i=100\text{ m}
\]

This suggests that Roof Height could be defined using either a **maximum** or a **supremum**.

---

# 26. The Roof Height Ceiling

A stricter version of the concept defines Roof Height independently of existing objects.

For example:

\[
H_R=1,000\text{ m}
\]

is established first.

Then every object must satisfy:

\[
H_i\leq1,000\text{ m}
\]

In this version, Roof Height is a **constraint** rather than merely an observation.

This is arguably the stronger theoretical formulation.

---

# 27. Static Roof Height

A Roof Height can be static.

If:

\[
H_R(t)=100\text{ m}
\]

for every time \(t\), the boundary never changes.

Buildings and trees may grow or be constructed, but none can exceed the boundary.

This creates a temporally stable environment.

---

# 28. Dynamic Roof Height

Alternatively:

\[
H_R=H_R(t)
\]

could change over time.

For example:

\[
H_R(2020)=100\text{ m}
\]

\[
H_R(2050)=150\text{ m}
\]

\[
H_R(2100)=200\text{ m}
\]

This would mean that the maximum vertical domain expands over time.

However, the user's original concept is most naturally interpreted as a fixed universal boundary.

---

# 29. The Perfectly Empty Upper Region

The phrase **fully empty** is stronger than simply saying "no buildings."

If interpreted literally, the upper region contains none of the considered physical objects.

The distinction matters.

An environment might have no buildings above the Roof Height while still containing:

- clouds,
- birds,
- aircraft,
- balloons,
- smoke,
- rain,
- atmospheric gases.

That would not be complete emptiness.

Therefore, the strongest version of Roof Height requires the upper region to be empty of all relevant matter or objects defined by the model.

---

# 30. Physical Versus Conceptual Emptiness

In real physics, completely empty space is difficult to define absolutely.

Even interplanetary and interstellar space contains extremely sparse matter and fields.

Therefore, "fully empty" should be interpreted as a **model assumption**.

The concept can define the upper region as:

> Empty for all purposes of the Roof Height model.

This allows the thought experiment to remain mathematically clean without claiming that actual physical vacuum behaves this way.

---

# 31. Roof Height and the Sky

In ordinary life, the sky appears to have no roof.

The atmosphere gradually becomes thinner with altitude.

There is no ordinary horizontal ceiling separating the atmosphere from empty space.

Roof Height reverses this intuition.

The sky would effectively terminate at:

\[
z=H_R
\]

and beyond that boundary would begin the idealized empty region.

This would make the sky appear fundamentally different from Earth's real atmosphere.

---

# 32. Architectural Consequences

If Roof Height were a real environmental law, architecture would be profoundly affected.

Architects would have a maximum permitted building height.

For example:

\[
H_R=100\text{ m}
\]

would mean that no building could exceed 100 m.

This would affect:

- skyscraper design,
- floor counts,
- structural engineering,
- elevator systems,
- telecommunications,
- rooftop equipment,
- urban density,
- zoning regulations,
- emergency planning.

---

# 33. Floor-Count Constraint

Suppose an average floor requires:

\[
3.5\text{ m}
\]

and the Roof Height is:

\[
100\text{ m}
\]

Ignoring structural thickness and other constraints, the approximate maximum number of floors would be:

\[
\frac{100}{3.5}\approx28.57
\]

Therefore, approximately 28 floors could fit within the vertical limit.

In reality, the usable number would be lower because of:

- structural slabs,
- mechanical floors,
- roof systems,
- foundations,
- equipment,
- safety requirements.

Roof Height would therefore impose a direct architectural constraint.

---

# 34. Urban Density

A vertical restriction would influence how cities expand.

Without a vertical limit, cities can increase capacity through taller buildings.

With Roof Height:

\[
H\leq H_R
\]

vertical expansion eventually stops.

The remaining ways to accommodate growth would include:

- increasing building footprint,
- increasing land-use efficiency,
- reducing unused spaces,
- increasing underground construction,
- improving public transportation,
- developing surrounding regions.

Thus Roof Height could produce a more horizontally distributed urban morphology.

---

# 35. Trees Under Roof Height

Urban forestry would also be constrained.

A tree species capable of reaching 120 m could not grow naturally beyond a 100 m Roof Height in a strict model.

It might:

- stop growing,
- bend,
- branch horizontally,
- remain artificially trimmed,
- or otherwise conform to the boundary.

This introduces an unusual ecological constraint.

---

# 36. Forest Canopies

A forest could develop a canopy approaching the Roof Height.

Suppose:

\[
H_R=60\text{ m}
\]

and many trees approach:

\[
H_T\approx60\text{ m}
\]

The upper surface of the forest could form a complex canopy just beneath the universal boundary.

However, the Roof Height itself remains perfectly flat even if the forest canopy is irregular.

This produces two different surfaces:

1. **Natural canopy surface**
2. **Idealized Roof Height plane**

---

# 37. Roof Height and Mountains

Mountains make the concept especially dramatic.

If the Roof Height is 5,000 m, then mountains can extend only to 5,000 m.

A mountain could reach:

\[
4,999\text{ m}
\]

leaving only 1 m between its summit and the Roof Height.

A 5,000 m summit would touch the boundary.

This could create a world where mountain peaks appear to approach an invisible universal ceiling.

---

# 38. A Roof Height Map

A map could represent Roof Height as a scalar value:

\[
R(x,y)
\]

For a perfectly flat universal Roof Height:

\[
R(x,y)=C
\]

where \(C\) is constant.

For example:

\[
R(x,y)=500
\]

means every horizontal coordinate has the same upper boundary of 500 m.

This makes Roof Height a particularly simple mathematical surface.

---

# 39. The Roof Height Plane

In Cartesian coordinates, the plane is:

\[
z=C
\]

Its normal vector is:

\[
\vec n=(0,0,1)
\]

This means the plane is horizontal.

Its geometry is independent of \(x\) and \(y\).

That is exactly what makes it "flat."

---

# 40. Vertical Occupancy Ratio

One could define a **Vertical Occupancy Ratio**:

\[
VOR_i=\frac{H_i}{H_R}
\]

For example, if:

\[
H_i=50\text{ m}
\]

and:

\[
H_R=100\text{ m}
\]

then:

\[
VOR=0.5
\]

or:

\[
50\%
\]

The object uses half of the available vertical range.

A 90 m tower would have:

\[
VOR=0.9
\]

or:

\[
90\%
\]

---

# 41. Roof Proximity

Another useful concept is **Roof Proximity**:

\[
P_i=H_R-H_i
\]

If:

\[
H_R=100\text{ m}
\]

and:

\[
H_i=95\text{ m}
\]

then:

\[
P_i=5\text{ m}
\]

The object is only 5 m below the Roof Height.

This could be useful in analyzing urban structures.

---

# 42. Roof-Touching Index

A normalized index could be defined:

\[
RTI_i=\frac{H_i}{H_R}
\]

Then:

- \(RTI=0\): ground-level object
- \(RTI=0.5\): half the Roof Height
- \(RTI=0.9\): near the boundary
- \(RTI=1\): exactly touching the boundary

Objects with:

\[
RTI=1
\]

could be called **Roof-Touching Objects**.

---

# 43. Roof Height and Engineering

Engineering would have to treat Roof Height as a hard boundary condition.

A structure's design constraints would include:

\[
H\leq H_R
\]

This could simplify some design problems while making others more difficult.

Engineers could no longer solve vertical constraints simply by increasing height.

Instead, they would optimize:

- footprint,
- structural efficiency,
- floor area,
- underground volume,
- horizontal transportation,
- interior layout.

---

# 44. Telecommunications

Communication towers are often built high because height improves line-of-sight coverage.

Roof Height would restrict this.

If:

\[
H_R=50\text{ m}
\]

a tower could not exceed 50 m.

Therefore, engineers would need alternatives such as:

- denser networks,
- more distributed towers,
- underground infrastructure combined with shorter antennas,
- satellite communication,
- alternative transmission technologies.

Thus a universal vertical boundary would affect communication architecture.

---

# 45. Transportation

Air transportation introduces another interesting question.

If aircraft are considered "objects," then aircraft cannot exist above the Roof Height.

This would fundamentally change aviation.

For example, if:

\[
H_R=200\text{ m}
\]

ordinary aircraft could not operate at conventional cruising altitudes.

Air travel would therefore be impossible in its familiar form unless aircraft were exempt from the definition.

This demonstrates why a complete theory of Roof Height must specify the **object domain**.

---

# 46. Scope of the Definition

A Roof Height model should therefore answer:

> What objects are included?

Possible categories include:

### Model A — Structures Only

Buildings, towers, trees, poles, and other stationary objects.

### Model B — All Physical Objects

Everything, including aircraft and moving objects.

### Model C — Permanent Environmental Objects

Only objects that permanently occupy the environment.

### Model D — All Matter

An extremely strict interpretation.

Each produces a different system.

---

# 47. Permanent Roof Height

A particularly useful definition is:

> Roof Height is the maximum permitted elevation of permanent or semi-permanent environmental structures.

This allows temporary phenomena such as:

- birds,
- aircraft,
- balloons,
- weather systems

to be treated separately.

This makes the concept more practical for urban and geographic analysis.

---

# 48. Roof Height as a Thought Experiment

The concept is especially powerful as a thought experiment.

It asks what would happen if the vertical dimension of civilization had an absolute limit.

Human civilization normally operates under a practical vertical limit determined by:

- engineering,
- economics,
- geography,
- materials,
- regulations.

Roof Height replaces these complicated constraints with one idealized rule:

\[
H\leq H_R
\]

This allows researchers and thinkers to explore the consequences of a universal vertical boundary.

---

# 49. Philosophical Interpretation

The concept can also be interpreted philosophically.

Human beings generally perceive "upward" as open-ended.

There is always apparently more sky.

Roof Height introduces the idea that even vertical space can have a boundary.

It therefore transforms the conceptual structure of the environment from:

> ground → increasingly high space → sky

into:

> ground → occupied vertical world → Roof Height → absolute emptiness.

The idea is similar to placing a boundary on a dimension that normally appears unlimited.

# 72. Planetary Scale

# 75. Roof Height and Coordinate Systems

- a local datum,
- a building foundation.

A rigorous Roof Height specification must therefore include a **vertical datum**.

# 76. A Formal Definition

A rigorous version could be written as follows:

> Given a defined spatial domain \(D\) and vertical datum \(z=0\), the Roof Height \(H_R\) is a constant \(C\) such that all permitted persistent objects \(O_i\subset D\) satisfy \(z\leq C\), while the region \(z>C\) contains no permitted objects.

Thus:

\[
H_R=C
\]

and:

\[
\forall O_i,\quad \max(z(O_i))\leq C
\]

while:

\[
z>C\Rightarrow \text{empty}
\]

within the model.

# 77. Core Properties

A canonical Roof Height system has the following properties:

1. **Verticality** — it concerns the upward dimension.
2. **Universality** — it applies to all defined objects.
3. **Constancy** — the boundary has a fixed elevation.
4. **Flatness** — the boundary is geometrically planar.
5. **Emptiness above** — the region beyond it contains no permitted objects.
6. **Object independence** — the boundary is not merely one building's roof.
7. **Environmental scope** — it applies to a defined geographic or conceptual domain.
8. **Mathematical expressibility** — it can be represented by a constant \(z=C\).

# 107. Conclusion

**Roof Height** is a conceptual framework for describing an environment with a universal upper vertical limit.

Under the strongest form of the concept:

\[
\boxed{H_i\leq H_R}
\]

for every included object, while:

\[
\boxed{z>H_R}
\]

contains no permitted objects.

The Roof Height itself is represented by a flat horizontal plane:

\[
\boxed{z=H_R}
\]

The world below it contains buildings, trees, terrain, infrastructure, and other objects.

The world above it is completely empty within the assumptions of the model.

The concept is therefore more than simply the height of a roof. It is an abstract **vertical boundary condition for an entire environment**.

It can be used to imagine cities that cannot grow indefinitely upward, forests whose trees cannot exceed a universal level, mountains that terminate beneath an invisible ceiling, virtual worlds with mathematically bounded vertical dimensions, and fictional universes in which the sky itself has a defined upper limit.

At its simplest, Roof Height can be reduced to one equation:

\[
\boxed{z\leq H_R}
\]

Everything that belongs to the occupied world exists at or below that level.

Above it:

\[
\boxed{z>H_R}
\]

there is only empty space.

Thus, the defining idea of Roof Height can be summarized in one sentence:

> **Roof Height is the universal flat upper boundary of an environment, where every included vertical object stops at or below it and everything above it is completely empty.**

The concept transforms the ordinary idea of a roof from the top of a single structure into the imagined top of an entire world.
