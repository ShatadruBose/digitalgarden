# Why These Shapes Are the Best: A Deep Study of the Square, Triangle, Circle, and Hexagon

## Abstract

Among the countless geometric forms that exist in mathematics, nature, engineering, architecture, art, science, and everyday life, four shapes stand out as particularly fundamental and powerful:

1. Square.
2. Triangle.
3. Circle.
4. Hexagon.

Calling these shapes "the best" is not a statement that every other shape is inferior. Rather, these four represent four extraordinary geometric ideas: **order, structural stability, continuity, and efficient packing**. Each possesses properties that make it exceptionally useful for particular purposes.

The square represents symmetry, regularity, organization, and perpendicular structure. The triangle is the great structural shape: rigid, stable, and mathematically foundational. The circle represents perfect rotational symmetry, smoothness, and constant distance from a center. The hexagon combines symmetry with remarkable packing efficiency and appears repeatedly in both natural and human-made systems.

Together, these four shapes form an impressive geometric toolkit. They can construct buildings, machines, diagrams, games, patterns, artworks, scientific models, computer graphics, molecules, maps, cells, wheels, tiles, and countless other systems.

The deeper lesson is that there is no single universal "best" shape. A shape becomes best when its geometric properties match the problem being solved. The square is excellent for organization. The triangle is excellent for strength. The circle is excellent for rotation. The hexagon is excellent for efficient packing.

This paper examines why each of these shapes deserves a place among geometry's greatest forms.

---

# 1. Introduction: What Does "Best" Mean in Geometry?

Geometry contains an enormous variety of shapes.

There are polygons with three sides, four sides, five sides, six sides, seven sides, eight sides, and infinitely many possible numbers of sides. There are curves, spirals, ellipses, parabolas, irregular figures, fractals, polyhedra, and complicated mathematical objects.

So how can four shapes be called "the best"?

The answer depends on **criteria**.

A shape can be considered excellent because it is:

- mathematically simple;
- highly symmetrical;
- structurally stable;
- efficient;
- easy to construct;
- easy to calculate;
- easy to recognize;
- useful in engineering;
- common in nature;
- useful for tiling;
- useful for computation;
- visually pleasing;
- easy to combine with other shapes;
- fundamental to mathematics.

By these standards, the square, triangle, circle, and hexagon are exceptionally strong candidates.

They are not merely shapes that children learn early in school. They are deeply embedded in advanced mathematics and the physical world.

# 2. The Four Champions

The four shapes can be thought of as representing four different geometric strengths.

| Shape | Main Strength | Famous Property |
|---|---|---|
| Square | Order and organization | Four equal sides and four right angles |
| Triangle | Structural stability | A rigid polygon with three sides |
| Circle | Rotational perfection | Every point on the circumference is equally distant from the center |
| Hexagon | Efficient packing | Regular hexagons tile the plane and use space efficiently |

This gives us a fascinating geometric quartet.

The square is the **organizer**.

The triangle is the **builder**.

The circle is the **rotator**.

The hexagon is the **packer**.

# 3. The Square

## 3.1 What Is a Square?

A square is a quadrilateral with:

- four equal sides;
- four right angles;
- opposite sides parallel;
- diagonals of equal length;
- diagonals that bisect one another;
- diagonals that are perpendicular;
- diagonals that bisect the interior angles.

Its interior angles are all:

\[
90^\circ
\]

and the sum of its interior angles is:

\[
360^\circ
\]

The square is simultaneously:

- a rectangle;
- a rhombus;
- a parallelogram;
- a regular polygon.

That makes it unusually versatile.

---

# 4. Why the Square Is One of the Best Shapes

## 4.1 Perfect Regularity

The square has an extraordinary level of regularity.

Every side has the same length.

Every angle is the same.

Every corner behaves identically.

There is no "special" corner.

There is no "long side" or "short side."

Everything is balanced.

This makes the square extremely easy to understand and manipulate.

---

## 4.2 The Square Is Excellent for Organization

Humans naturally use grids to organize information.

A grid is fundamentally based on perpendicular horizontal and vertical lines, and squares arise automatically from their intersections.

This is why squares appear in:

- graph paper;
- calendars;
- spreadsheets;
- chessboards;
- pixel grids;
- floor plans;
- maps;
- tables;
- tiled floors;
- windows;
- boards;
- UI layouts.

The square transforms continuous space into an organized system.

A huge complicated area can be divided into thousands or millions of smaller squares.

---

# 5. The Square and Tiling

One of the square's greatest powers is its ability to tile a plane.

Place identical squares next to one another.

There are no gaps.

There are no overlaps.

The result is an infinite grid.

This is mathematically important and practically useful.

Square tiling appears in:

- flooring;
- brick-like construction systems;
- pixel displays;
- circuit-board layouts;
- graph paper;
- city planning;
- computer graphics.

Few shapes are as naturally suited to rectangular organization.

# 6. The Square in Computing

Modern computing is filled with squares and rectangles.

A digital image can be represented as a grid of pixels.

Each pixel is approximately a tiny square.

A 1920 × 1080 image therefore contains:

\[
1920 \times 1080
\]

pixel positions.

The square becomes a fundamental unit for representing two-dimensional digital information.

Even computer interfaces rely heavily on rectangular and square structures.

Buttons, panels, cards, windows, grids, icons, thumbnails, and layout systems frequently use rectangular geometry.

The square is extremely important in architecture because right angles are easy to construct and measure.

Walls can meet at:

90^\circ

Furniture can fit against walls.

Floor tiles can be arranged efficiently.

Buildings can be divided into repeating modules.

The square therefore represents **architectural order**.

# 8. The Square and Symmetry

A square has multiple symmetry operations.

It possesses:

- four lines of reflection symmetry;
- rotational symmetry of order 4.

It looks identical after rotations of:

\[
90^\circ,\ 180^\circ,\ 270^\circ,\ 360^\circ
\]

This makes the square a beautiful example of symmetry in mathematics.

\[
P=4s
\]

### Area

\[
A=s^2
\]

### Diagonal

\[
d=s\sqrt{2}

When you want a system that is predictable, modular, measurable, and organized, the square is difficult to beat.

# 11. The Triangle

If the square is the organizer, the triangle is the structural champion.

The triangle is arguably the most important polygon in engineering.

Why?

Because triangles are extraordinarily stable.

---

# 12. What Is a Triangle?

A triangle is a polygon with:

- three sides;
- three vertices;
- three interior angles.

The interior angles always add to:

\[
180^\circ
\]

A triangle can be:

- equilateral;
- isosceles;
- scalene;
- acute;
- right-angled;
- obtuse.

Despite their different appearances, all triangles share fundamental structural properties.

---

# 13. Why the Triangle Is One of the Best Shapes

## 13.1 Structural Rigidity

Consider a square made from four rods.

Push one corner.

The square can deform into a parallelogram.

The side lengths can remain unchanged while the angles change.

Now consider a triangle.

If its three side lengths are fixed, its shape is fixed.

This property is called **rigidity**.

That is an enormous advantage in construction.

---

Triangles appear everywhere in structures.

Examples include:

- cranes;
- transmission towers;

- geodesic structures;
- structural frameworks.

Instead of relying on large solid pieces, engineers can create frameworks using interconnected triangles.

This produces structures that can be both strong and relatively lightweight.

# 15. The Triangle and Force Distribution

A triangle provides an efficient way to transfer forces through a framework.

Forces can travel through the members of a triangular truss primarily as:

- tension;
- compression.

This makes triangular structures highly useful in civil and mechanical engineering.

A triangular truss can distribute loads across multiple members instead of concentrating everything at one point.

# 16. The Triangle Is the Foundation of Geometry

Triangles are not merely one topic in geometry.

They are fundamental to geometry itself.

Many complex polygons can be divided into triangles.

For a polygon with \(n\) sides, the number of triangles produced by drawing diagonals from one vertex is:

\[
n-2
\]

Thus:

- triangle → 1 triangle;
- quadrilateral → 2 triangles;
- pentagon → 3 triangles;
- hexagon → 4 triangles;
- octagon → 6 triangles.

This allows complicated shapes to be analyzed using triangles.

---

# 18. The Right Triangle

The right triangle deserves special recognition.

Its defining angle is:

\[
90^\circ
\]

The famous Pythagorean theorem states:

\[
a^2+b^2=c^2
\]

where \(c\) is the hypotenuse.

This theorem is one of the most important relationships in mathematics.

It allows distances to be calculated from perpendicular components.

# 19. Triangles in Computer Graphics

Computer graphics heavily rely on triangles.

Three-dimensional objects are commonly represented as meshes consisting of triangular faces.

Why triangles?

Because any three non-collinear points define a plane.

Four or more points can potentially fail to be coplanar.

Triangles are therefore mathematically convenient for representing surfaces.

Modern 3D rendering pipelines are consequently built around triangular primitives.

# 20. Triangles in Navigation and Location

Triangulation and related geometric techniques allow positions to be determined from known reference points.

# 21. The Triangle's Great Advantage

The triangle's greatest strength is:

If you want something that resists deformation and provides a reliable geometric framework, the triangle is one of the best choices possible.

# 22. The Circle

The circle is radically different from the square and triangle.

The square has corners.

The triangle has corners.

The circle has none.

Instead, it provides continuous curvature.

---

# 23. What Is a Circle?

A circle is the set of all points in a plane that are a fixed distance from a particular point called the center.

That fixed distance is the radius.

If the radius is \(r\), then every point on the circumference is exactly \(r\) units from the center.

This gives the circle extraordinary symmetry.

---

# 24. Why the Circle Is One of the Best Shapes

## 24.1 Perfect Rotational Symmetry

A circle looks identical after rotation through **any angle**.

Rotate it:

\[
1^\circ
\]

or:

\[
37^\circ
\]

or:

\[
123.456^\circ
\]

or any other angle.

It remains visually identical.

This is stronger rotational symmetry than a square, triangle, or hexagon.

---

# 25. The Circle and Wheels

The circle is perhaps the greatest shape for rotational motion.

Imagine a wheel that is not circular.

Its center-to-edge distance would change as it rotated.

The object would move irregularly.

A circle maintains a constant radius.

Therefore a circular wheel can roll smoothly while keeping its center at a constant height above the ground.

This is one reason circular geometry is fundamental to transportation.

---

# 26. Circles in Machines

Circles appear in:

- wheels;

# 27. The Circle and Efficiency

For a fixed perimeter, the circle encloses the greatest possible area among all planar shapes.

Conversely, for a fixed area, the circle has the smallest perimeter.

This property is related to the **isoperimetric problem**.

It explains why circles are important when minimizing boundary length or maximizing enclosed area.

# 29. The Circle and Surface Tension

A liquid droplet tends toward a spherical shape because surface tension tends to minimize surface area for a given volume.

A sphere is the three-dimensional analogue of the circle.

Thus circular and spherical geometry appear naturally in physics.

\]

\[

The fundamental formulas include:

\[

\]

### Area

\[

\]

The circle therefore connects geometry with one of the most important constants in mathematics.

# 32. The Circle's Great Advantage

The circle's greatest strength is:

When rotation, smooth motion, constant distance, or minimum boundary is important, the circle is exceptionally powerful.

# 33. The Hexagon

Now we reach the most fascinating member of the quartet.

The regular hexagon is particularly impressive.

# 34. What Is a Hexagon?

A hexagon is a polygon with six sides.

A regular hexagon has:

- six equal sides;
- six equal angles;
- six vertices;
- six lines of symmetry;
- rotational symmetry of order 6.

Each interior angle of a regular hexagon is:

\[
120^\circ
\]

because:

\[
\frac{(6-2)180^\circ}{6}=120^\circ
\]

---

# 35. Why the Hexagon Is One of the Best Shapes

The hexagon has an extraordinary ability to combine **packing efficiency and structural usefulness**.

Regular hexagons can tile a flat surface perfectly.

Put them together:

- no gaps;
- no overlaps;
- continuous coverage.

This makes the hexagon a major geometric solution to packing problems.

---

# 36. The Honeycomb

The most famous natural example is the honeycomb.

Honeybees construct hexagonal cells.

The geometry is remarkable because the hexagonal pattern provides:

- efficient use of space;
- repeated modular cells;
- shared walls;
- structural organization.

The honeycomb has become one of the most famous examples of geometry appearing in biology.

---

# 37. The Hexagon and Efficient Tiling

Three regular polygons can tile the plane by themselves:

- equilateral triangles;
- squares;
- regular hexagons.

The regular hexagon is especially interesting because it produces large cells while maintaining a highly symmetric structure.

Its interior angle is:

\[
120^\circ
\]

Three hexagons can meet at a vertex:

\[
120^\circ+120^\circ+120^\circ=360^\circ
\]

Therefore the plane can be covered perfectly.

---

# 38. The Hexagon and Material Efficiency

A hexagonal pattern can create structures with a high strength-to-weight ratio.

This idea is used in:

- aerospace structures;
- sandwich panels;
- packaging;
- architecture;
- lightweight materials;
- engineering cores.

A honeycomb core can provide substantial stiffness without requiring a completely solid block of material.

This is a major engineering advantage.

---

# 39. Hexagons in Chemistry

Hexagonal geometry appears frequently in chemistry.

Carbon atoms can form hexagonal networks.

Graphene is a famous example.

Its carbon atoms form a two-dimensional honeycomb lattice.

Graphene's structure contributes to its extraordinary properties, including:

- high electrical conductivity;
- high thermal conductivity;
- exceptional mechanical strength;
- very low mass per unit area.

The hexagon is therefore connected to advanced materials science.

# 40. Hexagons in Biology

Nature often uses repeated geometric units because repeated structures can be efficient and stable.

# 41. Hexagons in Games and Maps

Hexagonal grids are particularly useful when movement occurs in many directions.

This makes hexagonal grids useful in:

- strategy games;

- spatial simulations;
- cellular automata;
- map design.

Hexagonal grids often produce more uniform neighborhood relationships than square grids.

It manages to be highly regular while also fitting together beautifully with identical copies.

- right;
