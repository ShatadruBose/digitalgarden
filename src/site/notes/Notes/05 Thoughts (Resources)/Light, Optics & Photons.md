# Light, Optics & Photons

> [!abstract] Overview
> Light is electromagnetic radiation with both wave-like and quantum properties. Optics is the study of light and its interactions with matter. A photon is the quantum of the electromagnetic field. Together, these concepts connect electromagnetism, quantum physics, chemistry, biology, astronomy, photography, and photonics.

---

# 1. Light

Light is electromagnetic radiation. In everyday usage, light usually means **visible light**, but electromagnetic radiation extends far beyond the visible spectrum.

Electromagnetic radiation consists of oscillating electric and magnetic fields. The fields are perpendicular to each other and to the direction of propagation.

In vacuum:

$$
c = 299\,792\,458\ \text{m/s}
$$

Approximately:

$$
c \approx 3.00\times10^8\ \text{m/s}
$$

Light does not require a material medium to propagate.

---

# 2. Electromagnetic Spectrum

The electromagnetic spectrum is the complete range of electromagnetic radiation.

```text
Long wavelength                                      Short wavelength
Low frequency                                        High frequency
Low photon energy                                    High photon energy

Radio → Microwave → Infrared → Visible → UV → X-ray → Gamma
```

## Major regions

| Region | General characteristic |
|---|---|
| Radio | Long wavelength, low frequency |
| Microwave | Communication, radar, heating |
| Infrared | Thermal radiation, sensing |
| Visible | Detected by the human eye |
| Ultraviolet | Higher-energy radiation beyond violet |
| X-ray | High-energy penetrating radiation |
| Gamma | Extremely high-energy electromagnetic radiation |

All of these are electromagnetic radiation; what changes is primarily their frequency and wavelength.

# 3. Wave Properties of Light

Light has several important wave properties.

### Wavelength

Wavelength ($\lambda$) is the spatial period of a wave.

### Frequency

Frequency ($f$) is the number of oscillations per second.

$$
f=\frac{1}{T}
$$

### Period

The period ($T$) is the time required for one complete oscillation.

### Amplitude

Amplitude describes the magnitude of the electromagnetic field oscillation.

### Phase

Phase describes the position of a wave within its oscillation cycle.

The fundamental wave relationship is:

$$
c=f\lambda
$$

Therefore:

$$
f=\frac{c}{\lambda}
$$

Higher frequency means shorter wavelength.

# 4. Photons

A **photon** is the quantum of the electromagnetic field.

A photon has:

- Energy
- Momentum
- Angular momentum (spin)
- No rest mass
- No electric charge

The energy of a photon is:

$$
E=hf
$$

Since:

$$
c=f\lambda
$$

we obtain:

$$
E=\frac{hc}{\lambda}
$$

Therefore:

```text
Higher frequency
      ↓
Higher photon energy

Shorter wavelength
      ↓
Higher photon energy
```

A photon also has momentum:

$$
p=\frac{h}{\lambda}
$$

Light exhibits both wave-like and quantum behavior.

## Wave phenomena

- Interference
- Diffraction

- Reflection
- Dispersion

## Quantum phenomena

- Photoelectric effect
- Photon emission

The modern description of light is quantum electromagnetic theory, with **quantum electrodynamics (QED)** providing the fundamental framework for electromagnetic interactions.

# 6. Optics

**Optics** is the branch of physics concerned with light, its propagation, its interaction with matter, and optical systems.

It can broadly be divided into:

```text
OPTICS
│
├── Geometrical Optics
│
├── Wave/Physical Optics
│
├── Quantum Optics
│
└── Nonlinear Optics
```

Geometrical optics treats light approximately as rays.

Wave optics treats light as a wave.

Quantum optics treats electromagnetic radiation and its interactions using quantum mechanics.

# 7. Reflection

**Reflection** occurs when light encounters a surface and returns into its original medium.

The fundamental law is:

$$
\theta_i=\theta_r
$$

where:

- $\theta_i$ = angle of incidence
- $\theta_r$ = angle of reflection

The angles are measured relative to the **normal**, not the surface.

## Types of reflection

### Regular reflection

Occurs primarily on smooth surfaces.

### Diffuse reflection

Occurs when a rough surface scatters reflected rays in many directions.

---

# 8. Refraction

**Refraction** is the change in propagation direction associated with light entering a different medium.

The refractive index is:

$$
n=\frac{c}{v}
$$

where:

- $n$ = refractive index
- $c$ = speed of light in vacuum
- $v$ = phase velocity in the medium

Refraction occurs because the phase velocity of light changes between media.

Snell's law is:

$$
n_1\sin\theta_1=n_2\sin\theta_2
$$

---

# 9. Total Internal Reflection & Dispersion

## Total Internal Reflection

Total internal reflection occurs when light travels from a higher-index medium toward a lower-index medium and the incident angle exceeds the critical angle.

For $n_1>n_2$:

$$
\sin\theta_c=\frac{n_2}{n_1}
$$

Applications include:

- Optical fibers
- Prisms
- Endoscopes
- Optical sensors

## Dispersion

Dispersion occurs when different wavelengths behave differently in a material.

A prism can separate white light into its constituent colors because different wavelengths experience different refractive indices.

---

# 10. Mirrors

Mirrors are optical surfaces that primarily use reflection to form images.

## Plane mirror

Produces a generally:

- Virtual image
- Upright image
- Same-size image
- Laterally inverted image

## Concave mirror

A converging mirror capable of producing real or virtual images depending on object position.

## Convex mirror

A diverging mirror that generally produces virtual, upright, reduced images.

The mirror equation is commonly written as:

$$
\frac{1}{f}=\frac{1}{u}+\frac{1}{v}
$$

The signs of $u$, $v$, and $f$ depend on the chosen sign convention.

---

# 11. Lenses

A lens is a transparent optical element that refracts light.

## Convex lens

A converging lens, generally thicker in the middle.

## Concave lens

A diverging lens, generally thinner in the middle.

For a thin lens:

$$
\frac{1}{f}
=
\frac{1}{d_o}
+
\frac{1}{d_i}
$$

where:

- $f$ = focal length
- $d_o$ = object distance
- $d_i$ = image distance

Magnification is:

$$
m=\frac{h_i}{h_o}
$$

and, under a common sign convention:

$$
m=-\frac{d_i}{d_o}
$$

# 12. Interference & Diffraction

## Interference

Interference occurs when waves overlap and their amplitudes combine.

### Constructive interference

Waves reinforce each other.

### Destructive interference

Waves partially or completely cancel.

Young's double-slit experiment demonstrates the interference of light.

## Diffraction

Diffraction is the spreading and interference of waves around obstacles and through apertures.

It becomes especially important when the dimensions of an aperture or obstacle are comparable to the wavelength.

# 13. Polarization & Coherence

## Polarization

Polarization describes the orientation of the electric-field oscillation.

Types include:

- Linear polarization
- Circular polarization
- Elliptical polarization

Applications:

- Polarizing sunglasses
- LCDs
- Photography
- Microscopy

## Coherence

Coherence describes stable phase relationships between waves.

Two major types are:

- Temporal coherence
- Spatial coherence

Coherence is important in:

- Lasers
- Interferometry
- Holography
- Precision measurements

# 15. Emission, Fluorescence & Phosphorescence

## Spontaneous emission

An excited atom or molecule can spontaneously transition to a lower energy state and emit a photon.

## Stimulated emission

An incoming photon can stimulate an excited system to emit another photon with closely related quantum properties.

Stimulated emission is the basis of laser operation.

## Fluorescence

Fluorescence is relatively prompt light emission following excitation.

## Phosphorescence

Phosphorescence involves delayed emission that can continue after the excitation source is removed.

## Luminescence

Luminescence is light emission not primarily caused by high temperature.

Examples include:

- Fluorescence
- Phosphorescence
- Bioluminescence
- Electroluminescence
- Chemiluminescence

# 16. Scattering & Atmospheric Optics

## Rayleigh scattering

Rayleigh scattering occurs when scattering particles are much smaller than the wavelength.

Its wavelength dependence is approximately:

$$
I\propto\frac{1}{\lambda^4}
$$

This helps explain the blue appearance of the daytime sky.

## Mie scattering

Mie scattering becomes important when particles are comparable to or larger than the wavelength.

It contributes strongly to phenomena involving clouds and atmospheric aerosols.

## Raman scattering

Raman scattering is an inelastic scattering process that provides information about molecular vibrations.

## Atmospheric phenomena

Light produces many atmospheric optical phenomena:

- Blue sky
- Red sunsets
- Rainbows
- Halos
- Sundogs
- Coronas
- Glories
- Mirages
- Crepuscular rays

# 17. Color & Vision

Color is a perceptual phenomenon produced by the interaction between electromagnetic radiation, materials, the visual system, and the brain.

Visible light occupies approximately:

$$
380\text{–}750\ \text{nm}
$$

The approximate sequence is:

```text
Violet → Blue → Green → Yellow → Orange → Red
```

## Additive color

Displays commonly use:

```text
Red + Green = Yellow
Green + Blue = Cyan
Blue + Red = Magenta
Red + Green + Blue = White
```

This is the basis of **RGB**.

## Subtractive color

Printing commonly uses:

```text
Cyan + Magenta + Yellow + Black
```

This is the **CMYK** model.

# 23. Spectroscopy & Thermal Radiation

## Spectroscopy

Spectroscopy studies how electromagnetic radiation interacts with matter.

Spectra can reveal:

- Chemical composition
- Temperature
- Density
- Motion
- Energy levels
- Magnetic properties

Atomic emission and absorption lines arise from quantized energy levels.

## Thermal radiation

Objects above absolute zero emit electromagnetic radiation.

At ordinary temperatures, much of the emission is infrared.

## Blackbody radiation

An ideal blackbody absorbs all incident electromagnetic radiation and emits radiation determined by its temperature.

### Wien's displacement law

$$
\lambda_{\max}T=b
$$

### Stefan–Boltzmann law

$$
\frac{P}{A}=\sigma T^4
$$

Hotter objects generally radiate more energy and have shorter peak wavelengths.

# 24. Photoelectric Effect & Quantum Phenomena

The **photoelectric effect** occurs when electromagnetic radiation causes electrons to be emitted from a material.

Einstein's photon explanation gives:

$$
K_{\max}=hf-\phi
$$

where:

- $K_{\max}$ = maximum electron kinetic energy
- $h$ = Planck constant
- $f$ = photon frequency
- $\phi$ = work function

Important quantum light phenomena include:

- Photoelectric effect
- Compton scattering
- Photon absorption
- Photon emission
- Pair production
- Quantum interference
- Photon entanglement

### Compton scattering

A photon can scatter from an electron and change wavelength:

$$
\Delta\lambda=
\frac{h}{m_ec}(1-\cos\theta)
$$

### Pair production

A sufficiently energetic photon can participate in the production of a particle–antiparticle pair, such as:

$$
\gamma\rightarrow e^-+e^+
$$

Momentum conservation normally requires interaction with another object.

# 25. Relativity, Astronomy & Light

Light is fundamental to modern astronomy and relativity.

## Special relativity

The speed of light in vacuum is invariant for inertial observers.

This leads to:

- Time dilation
- Length contraction
- Relativity of simultaneity
- Mass–energy equivalence

Einstein's famous equation is:

$$
E=mc^2
$$

## Doppler effect

Relative motion changes the observed frequency and wavelength of light.

An object moving away can produce:

```text
Longer observed wavelength
        ↓
Redshift
```

An object moving toward the observer can produce:

```text
Shorter observed wavelength
        ↓
Blueshift
```

## Gravitational lensing

Gravity can bend the path of light.

In general relativity, this is understood through the curvature of spacetime.

Astronomers use gravitational lensing to study:

- Galaxies
- Galaxy clusters
- Dark matter
- Distant objects
- Cosmology

Real optical systems can suffer from:

- Spherical aberration
- Chromatic aberration
- Coma
- Astigmatism
- Field curvature
- Distortion

In nonlinear optics, the material response is not simply proportional to the electromagnetic field.

Phenomena include:

- Second-harmonic generation
- Sum-frequency generation
- Difference-frequency generation
- Four-wave mixing
- Optical Kerr effect
- Self-focusing

## Quantum optics

Quantum optics studies light using quantum mechanics.

Topics include:

- Single photons
- Photon statistics
- Quantum states
- Squeezed light
- Entanglement
- Quantum interference
- Quantum measurement

Holography uses interference and diffraction to record and reconstruct optical information, including information related to wavefronts.
